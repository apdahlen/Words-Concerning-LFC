STEP 1: HAZARD IDENTIFICATION

Lexical Hazards

* “Nogen” is an older spelling of “noen” and means “some” or “a few.” In the title, “A Few Words” preserves the modest introductory form.

* “Opgaver” means “tasks,” “duties,” or “responsibilities.” Here it refers to the Church’s divinely given work rather than merely administrative assignments. “Tasks” retains the author’s direct wording.

* “Maalbevist” means consciously directed toward a definite goal. “Lidet maalbevist” should not be reduced simply to “unclear”; it criticizes prayer that lacks a definite spiritual aim.

* “Frelse” consistently denotes salvation from sin and perdition through Christ. It must not be weakened to “help,” “healing,” or “spiritual well-being.”

* “Opbyggelse” literally carries the image of building up. In this passage, the author explicitly rejects its reduction to pleasant religious feeling. “Edification” preserves both the theological term and the argument built around it.

* “Verden,” placed in quotation marks, refers to humanity outside believing fellowship with Christ. It does not simply mean the physical world.

* “Anger,” “omvendelse,” “tro,” and “helliggjørelse” are distinct doctrinal terms: repentance, conversion, faith, and sanctification. Their sequence must be preserved.

* “Salig hvile” means blessed or saving rest. It is not merely psychological calm.

* “Tilveiebragt” means procured, accomplished, or brought about. It points to salvation as an objective work completed through Christ’s atoning death.

* “Utnyttet” here does not carry the modern negative sense of exploiting something. It means availed of or received for one’s benefit.

* “Bøier sig til Gud med helt sind” denotes an undivided yielding of the person to God. “With an undivided mind” preserves the force of “helt sind.”

* “Hengivelsen” means surrender or yielding to God, not merely emotional devotion.

* “Aandens veiledning og virkning” refers to the Holy Spirit’s guidance and effective working. “Virkning” must retain the sense of divine operation rather than mere influence.

* “Syndserkjendelse” means knowledge or recognition of sin. It includes awakened conviction but should not be translated merely as guilt feelings.

* “Sindelag” means mind, disposition, or inward orientation. “The mind of Jesus” retains both its ethical and biblical resonance.

* “Forsagelsen” refers to renunciation and self-denial. It is connected with discipleship and the rejection of sin, self-will, and evil.

* “Usseldom” means wretchedness, misery, or moral-spiritual poverty. “Wretchedness” preserves its severity.

* “Gavnlig,” used of Christ’s yoke, means beneficial, wholesome, or good. Translating it automatically as the familiar “easy” would erase the distinctive wording of the Norwegian biblical form cited here.

* “Sabbatshelg” denotes the Sabbath rest reserved for God’s people. It carries the force of Hebrews 4:9 and points to eternal rest, not merely a religious holiday.

Syntactic Hazards

* The prose relies on long cumulative sentences in which the argument develops through contrasts, subordinate clauses, and delayed conclusions. Excessive sentence division would flatten the sermonic reasoning.

* The author repeatedly uses impersonal passive forms such as “der bedes” and “faaes.” These present the practices under criticism as widespread ecclesial habits rather than isolated personal acts.

* Several sentences pivot sharply through “Men,” “Nei,” “Ti,” and “altsaa.” These connectors carry the logical pressure of the argument and should remain explicit.

* The author moves between “the individual,” “the soul,” “I,” “we,” and “the believers.” These shifts progressively bring the argument from general observation to direct examination of conscience.

* Quoted religious expressions such as “salvation,” “edification,” “the world,” and “I have been converted” are placed under scrutiny. Their quotation marks must be retained.

* The sentence concerning “our fixed way and that life” is syntactically compressed. The intended sense is that believers must walk fearlessly on their appointed way, within a life whose wealth is joyful self-sacrifice.

* Biblical quotations preserve older sentence order and causal structure. They should not be rearranged into contemporary conversational English.

* The hymn interrupts prose argument with a lyrical confession. Its stanzaic structure and heightened cadence should remain distinct.

Theological Hazards

* Salvation is grounded outside the believer, in the completed atoning death of Jesus Christ. The translation must not shift the ground of assurance into the believer’s repentance, conversion, faith, sanctification, or feelings.

* The passage states that salvation has been procured for all but is received only by those who yield to God. This distinction between objective provision and personal reception must remain intact.

* Conversion is described as God’s own work through his Spirit and Word, accomplished despite human resistance. Any wording that makes conversion principally a human achievement would contradict the argument.

* The author distinguishes joy in the Lord from satisfaction with one’s religious condition. This is a doctrinal contrast between trust in Christ’s completed salvation and confidence in one’s own conversion.

* True edification deepens knowledge of sin, gratitude for salvation, and zeal for the salvation of others. It does not confirm the believer in spiritual self-approval.

* Discipleship is represented through Christ’s yoke, self-denial, daily cross-bearing, struggle, sacrifice, and costly service. These must not be softened into general personal growth.

* The author joins spiritual rest and active service: the soul finds rest precisely in taking Christ’s yoke and learning his mind.

* Grace does not remove struggle; it enables struggle against the self, personal wretchedness, and evil in the surrounding world.

* The final consolation rests in God’s unchangeable love in Christ and in the promised Sabbath rest with Christ after the battle is completed.

Voice Hazards

* The author’s criticism of self-satisfied Christianity is sharp but controlled. It should not be softened into a mild warning against complacency.

* The ironic statement that the world would be well if everyone were like “me” exposes spiritual pride. Its edge must remain.

* “It costs humiliation, sacrifice, money” is deliberately abrupt. Expanding or explaining it would weaken its force.

* The passage repeatedly implicates the reader through “I” and “we.” Translation into detached third-person academic prose would destroy the address to conscience.

* The author moves from rebuke to consolation without abandoning seriousness. The consolation must not become sentimental.

* The hymn expresses earnest submission rather than emotional exuberance. Its intensity comes from willingness to follow Christ through death, affliction, and storm.

* The concluding vision of eternal rest retains the marks of battle, cross-bearing, pilgrimage, and costly following. Hope does not cancel the severity of discipleship.

STEP 2: IDIOM ANALYSIS

* “To pray for ‘salvation’ for a soul” is revivalist and devotional shorthand. The author’s objection is not to prayer for salvation but to the absence of a definite understanding of what salvation entails.

* “Edification for the believers” is a conventional ecclesial expression. The author redefines it polemically: edification is not emotional comfort but a deeper exposure of sin, stronger reliance on grace, and increased zeal for others.

* “A pleasant satisfaction with oneself or with one’s Christianity” describes religious self-consciousness in which the believer takes pleasure in possessing a superior spiritual status.

* “The world” is a biblical-pietistic designation for those who remain outside the life of faith. The quotation marks show that the author is using an established religious category while criticizing the pride that may accompany it.

* “Joy in the Lord” is not inward delight in the success of one’s conversion. It is rest in salvation completed by Christ. The phrase echoes Nehemiah 8:10 and broader New Testament language concerning joy in the Lord.

* “The soul’s blessed rest” is not passivity. The later yoke imagery shows that rest is found within obedient discipleship.

* “Bow themselves to God with an undivided mind” denotes wholehearted yielding. A merely literal rendering such as “bend themselves” would sound physical and obscure the spiritual act.

* “Has something beyond the world” means that the believing soul possesses what the world lacks: salvation in Christ. The phrase does not authorize pride, since the change is immediately attributed to God alone.

* “I have been converted” is treated as a potentially self-congratulatory religious formula. The author does not deny conversion; he denies that joy should rest in contemplation of one’s converted condition.

* “Make salvation one’s own” is a pietistic expression for personally receiving and resting in the salvation procured by Christ. It must not imply that the believer creates or completes salvation.

* “The mind of Jesus” combines ethical conformity with Christ and likely echoes the exhortation to have the mind of Christ in Philippians 2:5.

* “It is my food to do the Father’s will” echoes Jesus’ words in John 4:34. “Food” signifies the sustaining joy and inward necessity of obedience.

* “Fight in self-denial” refers to active renunciation of self-will and sin. Translating it as merely “exercise restraint” would weaken its baptismal and discipleship associations.

* “The Lord’s army” is spiritual warfare imagery. It concerns struggle against evil within and around the believer, not physical or political violence.

* “Settle ourselves comfortably on earth” criticizes the desire to make earthly life secure and undisturbed while ignoring the suffering and spiritual need of others.

* “Crying need” personifies distress as calling aloud for a response. “Serious need” would lose the urgency.

* “World-liberating task” presents Christ’s work as directed toward the deliverance of the world. The military and liberation language remains spiritual and missionary.

* “Conquer the world for Christ” is missionary rhetoric. It signifies bringing the world under Christ’s saving rule through the gospel, not coercive domination.

* “The one journey they have through life” intensifies the ethical demand by stressing that discipleship cannot be postponed to another life.

* “The heart will writhe in pain” presents the cost of discipleship as inwardly agonizing. The physical image should remain.

* “Count everything as loss” echoes Philippians 3:8. The natural biblical English equivalent should be retained without adding archaic pronouns.

* “The way of the cross” unites discipleship, suffering, and pilgrimage. It should remain a metaphor rather than becoming “a difficult life.”

* “The foreign land” portrays earthly life as exile or pilgrimage away from the believer’s final homeland.

* “The Sabbath rest that remains for God’s people” closely echoes Hebrews 4:9. “Sabbath rest” is the appropriate English equivalent.

* “Where I am, there my servant will also be” is a direct Christological promise from John 12:26. Its simplicity and certainty should be preserved.

* Familiar King James forms such as “Take my yoke upon you,” “take up his cross,” “count all things loss,” and “Where I am” provide useful rhythmic models. Archaic pronouns and artificially antique diction, however, should be avoided.

STEP 3: ARGUMENT STRUCTURE IDENTIFICATION

* The author begins by extending an earlier criticism: prayer for “salvation” is often indefinite and lacking in a definite aim.

* He applies the same criticism to prayers for the “edification” of believers.

* He identifies the common but false understanding of edification as a pleasant feeling of satisfaction with oneself and one’s Christianity.

* He immediately qualifies the criticism: joy over salvation is not wrong. Joy in the Lord is strength.

* He then distinguishes true joy in the Lord from satisfaction with one’s repentance, conversion, faith, or sanctification.

* The proper ground of joy is the salvation completed through Jesus Christ’s atoning death, procured for all and personally received through the Spirit’s guidance and working.

* The author acknowledges that conversion is a real and immeasurably great change, but he denies that it is a ground for self-congratulation because it is God’s work through the Spirit and the Word.

* He emphasizes that salvation remains available to “the world,” since God wills all people to be saved.

* He returns to the definition of edification and states negatively what it is not: confirmation that one is thoroughly converted or spiritually superior.

* He defines edification positively as deeper knowledge of sin, greater gratitude for salvation, and more ardent zeal for the salvation of others.

* He deepens this definition through Christological imitation: edification means receiving more of Jesus’ mind, so that obedient struggle and service become the believer’s food, wealth, happiness, and secret joy.

* A rhetorical pivot follows: because true edification is costly, few genuinely desire or pray for it.

* The cost is named concretely: humiliation, sacrifice, and money.

* Christ’s invitation to take his yoke is then used to show that rest for the soul is found through obedient service, not withdrawal from it.

* The argument turns directly toward the collective conscience of the Church: believers are tempted to arrange a comfortable earthly life while minimizing concern for others’ crying need.

* The edification most needed in Christian gatherings is therefore courage and strength for joyful self-sacrifice in Christ’s world-liberating work.

* The author invokes 1 John 4:17 as the desired result: the life of Christ is to become visible and true in believers within this world.

* He restates the practical signs of true edification: deeper sorrow over one’s wretchedness, firmer reliance on grace, and stronger desire to join Christ’s mission.

* He intensifies the appeal by stressing the believer’s single passage through life and the necessity of following Jesus Christ now.

* Luke 9:23–24 supplies the controlling law of discipleship: self-denial, daily cross-bearing, and the losing of life in order to save it.

* The hymn gives personal and affective expression to the preceding doctrinal argument. The heart that has found salvation in Jesus willingly submits to struggle, danger, and affliction.

* The conclusion holds struggle and assurance together: believers fight by the grace given to them while rejoicing in God’s unchangeable love in Christ.

* The final movement looks beyond the completed battle and the traversed way of the cross toward the promised Sabbath rest with Christ.

* The concluding quotation seals the promise: Christ’s servant will be where Christ is.

STEP 4: BIBLICAL ECHO IDENTIFICATION

* “Joy in the Lord is our strength.”
  Likely source: Nehemiah 8:10, with the Norwegian wording using “in the Lord” rather than the more familiar English “of the Lord.”

* Salvation “procured for all” through Jesus Christ’s atoning death.
  This is a doctrinal synthesis rather than a direct quotation. Likely biblical foundations include Romans 3:24–25, 2 Corinthians 5:19, and 1 John 2:2.

* God’s work through his Spirit and Word in conversion.
  This reflects the Lutheran scriptural pattern found in passages such as John 3:5–8, Romans 10:17, and 1 Corinthians 12:3.

* “God wills that all people should be saved and come to the knowledge of the truth.”
  Direct or near-direct quotation from 1 Timothy 2:4.

* “The mind of Jesus.”
  Likely echo of Philippians 2:5: the call to have the mind that was in Christ Jesus.

* “My food” is to do the Father’s will.
  Direct echo of John 4:34, where Jesus says that his food is to do the will of the one who sent him and to finish his work.

* The Lord’s army and the struggle against evil.
  Likely general echo of New Testament spiritual warfare language, including Ephesians 6:10–17 and 2 Timothy 2:3–4.

* “Take my yoke upon you and learn from me … and you will find rest for your souls.”
  Direct quotation from Matthew 11:29–30.

* “As he is, so are we in this world.”
  Direct quotation from 1 John 4:17.

* Following Jesus through the single journey of life.
  This reflects the repeated Gospel call to immediate and undivided discipleship, especially Matthew 4:19–22, Luke 9:57–62, and John 12:26.

* “If anyone wishes to come after me, he must deny himself and every day take up his cross and follow me.”
  Direct quotation from Luke 9:23.

* “Whoever wishes to save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life for my sake will save it.”
  Direct quotation from Luke 9:24.

* “We count everything as loss.”
  Clear echo of Philippians 3:8.

* Earthly life as a foreign land.
  Likely echo of Hebrews 11:13–16 and 1 Peter 2:11, where believers are described as strangers, exiles, and pilgrims.

* The battle completed and the way traversed.
  Likely echoes of 2 Timothy 4:7 and other New Testament images of persevering to the end.

* “The Sabbath rest that remains for God’s people.”
  Direct echo of Hebrews 4:9.

* “Where I am, there my servant will also be.”
  Direct quotation from John 12:26.

STEP 5: VOICE INTENSITY PRESERVATION

* The opening criticism will remain direct: the prayers under discussion are “indefinite” and “lacking in a clear aim.”

* The false form of edification will be named as satisfaction with oneself and one’s Christianity, without substituting softer language such as “an inward sense of encouragement.”

* The contrast between rest in Christ and rest in one’s repentance, conversion, faith, or sanctification will remain explicit.

* The author’s insistence that conversion is God’s work despite human resistance will not be qualified or diluted.

* “How poor a Christian I am” and “my own powerlessness and wretchedness” will retain their accusatory force.

* The ironic claim that the world would be well if everyone were like “me” will remain intact.

* “It costs humiliation, sacrifice, money” will remain abrupt and concrete.

* The temptation to settle comfortably on earth while ignoring others’ crying need will remain a collective indictment: “we are all tempted.”

* The language of struggle, army, battle, conquest, self-denial, cross-bearing, flames, storm, and death will be retained.

* The concluding joy will remain grounded in God’s unchangeable love in Christ, not in optimism about human progress.

* The final promise will preserve certainty without theatrical embellishment.

STEP 6: MODAL FORCE PRESERVATION

* “Skal” in promises and consequences will be rendered with “will” or, where the declarative force requires it, “shall.” It will not be weakened to “may.”

* “Maa” will be translated as “must,” especially in Christ’s command that the disciple must deny himself.

* “Vil nogen komme efter mig” will be rendered “If anyone wishes to come after me,” preserving volition rather than treating the phrase as a simple future.

* “Den som vil berge sit liv” will remain “whoever wishes to save his life,” preserving the deliberate attempt at self-preservation.

* “Kan ikke” or equivalent negative necessity will remain “cannot,” not “does not usually.”

* “Det vil selvfølgelig koste” will remain a definite consequence: “It will, of course, cost.”

* “Det tør nok hænde” will be rendered “It may indeed happen,” preserving the author’s restrained acknowledgment of suffering.

* “Vi vil følge Jesus Kristus” will retain the force of conscious resolve: “we will follow Jesus Christ.”

* “Naar kun du … vil være” in the hymn will remain a condition of total dependence: “if only you … will be.”

* Christ’s promise, “there my servant will also be,” will retain its future certainty.

STEP 8: METAPHOR INTEGRITY PRESERVATION

* Salvation is something “procured,” “received,” “made one’s own,” “rested in,” and “built upon.” These distinct images will remain.

* Joy in the Lord is the soul’s “rest,” not merely a positive emotion.

* Christ’s obedient service is “food.” The metaphor of nourishment will not be explained away.

* The believer serves in “the Lord’s army” and fights evil within and around the self.

* Christ’s “yoke” and “burden” will remain concrete images of obedient discipleship.

* The need of others “cries” aloud.

* Christ’s task is “world-liberating,” and believers go out to “conquer the world for Christ.” The spiritual-missionary force of these images will be retained.

* The disciple must take up the “cross” every day.

* The heart “writhes” in pain at the cost.

* The hymn retains footsteps, battle, threatening death, flames of affliction, the storm, the wild sea, and the supporting staff.

* Earthly discipleship is a battle and a way of the cross through a foreign land.

* Eternal blessedness is represented as entry into the land of perfection and participation in the Sabbath rest.

STEP 9: TONE CONTRAST PRESERVATION

* Severity: The author exposes vagueness, religious self-satisfaction, and spiritual pride.

* Doctrinal consolation: Joy rests in salvation completed through Christ’s atoning death.

* Renewed severity: True edification reveals the believer’s poverty and wretchedness.

* Positive exhortation: The believer is called into Christlike service and concern for others.

* Costly demand: Humiliation, sacrifice, money, self-denial, and the daily cross are named without mitigation.

* Confessional devotion: The hymn answers the command of discipleship with willing submission to Jesus.

* Sustaining consolation: God’s unchangeable love in Christ lifts the heart during the struggle.

* Eschatological hope: The battle and pilgrimage end in the land of perfection and the Sabbath rest with Christ.

The translation will not equalize these movements into a uniformly solemn or uniformly comforting tone.

STEP 10: ORAL CADENCE AWARENESS

* Long sentences will be retained where the reasoning accumulates through successive clauses.

* Semicolons, colons, and dashes will preserve the author’s rhetorical pivots and breathing points.

* Repeated formulas such as “joy in the Lord,” “edification,” “the world,” and “the working of the Word” will remain audible as thematic refrains.

* Short declarations such as “On the contrary,” “No,” and “It is costly” will interrupt the longer argument with their original force.

* Biblical quotations will retain parallelism and consequence: command, reason, promise.

* The progression from “the soul” to “I” to “we” will remain, allowing the spoken argument to close upon the hearer’s conscience.

* The hymn will preserve short lyrical lines and parallel imperatives.

* The final paragraphs will retain their upward movement from battle, through assurance, to promised rest.

STEP 11: ACADEMIC TRANSLATION

[Page 242]

A Few Words on the Lutheran Free Church and Its Tasks

E. B.

[ornament]

IV.

Through the little I have said here about this matter, I have tried to indicate how indefinite and how lacking in a clear aim it is when prayer is offered for “salvation” for a soul.

And I believe that the same is true when prayer is offered for “edification for the believers.” For what is commonly meant by the expression “edification” is that the single person—the individual—may during the meeting be brought into a pleasant feeling of joy and satisfaction with himself or with his Christianity. In other words: joy and a sense of happiness in being a believer, in contrast to “the world.”

Now I do not mean to say that joy over salvation is unwarranted and harmful. On the contrary. I maintain, with God’s Word, that “joy in the Lord is our strength.” But in the first place, it is far from true that “joy in the Lord” is the same as a feeling of pleasant satisfaction with oneself and one’s conversion. No, “joy in the Lord” is the soul’s blessed rest—not in its repentance, conversion, faith, or sanctification, but in the completed salvation through Jesus Christ—procured for all, but received and availed of only by those who bow themselves to God with an undivided mind.

Now it is essentially true that the soul which “believes,” which receives in Jesus Christ salvation from sin and perdition, possesses something that “the world” does not. It is true that where surrender to God has taken place, a great change has occurred—the greatest that can happen to a human being. But this change, by which a person becomes willing to receive salvation, is a work of God himself through his Spirit and Word—a work which the Spirit has accomplished amid much resistance and many hindrances on our part.

“Joy in the Lord” cannot, therefore, be joy over the fact that “I have been converted,” but joy that salvation has been procured for me through Jesus’ atoning death; and when, through the Spirit’s guidance and working, I have learned to make it my own, to rest in it, and to build my whole blessedness upon it—then I possess something of the joy in the Lord. But the others—“the world”—can also receive a share in this “salvation.” For “God wills that all people should be saved and come to the knowledge of the truth.”

That the believers are edified is not the same as being confirmed in the thought that I am so thoroughly converted, or that in my heart I am certain that, if everyone were like me, all would be well in the world. No, “edification” is that, through the working of the Word, I am made to see how poor a Christian I am, how meager both faith and life are—in other words: that my knowledge of sin is deepened, my gratitude to God for salvation increased, and my zeal for the salvation of others made more ardent.

“Edification,” therefore, is this: that I receive more of the mind of Jesus, so that for me, as for him, it becomes “my food,” the wealth and happiness of my life, the secret fountain of joy in my soul, to be permitted to fight in self-denial, to take part in the Lord’s army in the battle against the evil within us—and around us.

But if this is the true “edification” of the believer, it is easy to understand that not very many are inclined to desire it, to pray for it. For it is costly. It costs humiliation, sacrifice, money.

Jesus says: “Take my yoke upon you and learn from me, for I am gentle and humble in heart, and you will find rest for your souls: for my yoke is beneficial and my burden is light.”

Here he says, therefore, that “his yoke” is the soul’s rest; that edification, rest for the soul, is obtained by taking “his yoke,” by learning from him to labor for his cause with something of that mind to which it is “food to do the Father’s will.”

Whether we will admit it or not, it is nevertheless true that we are all tempted to settle ourselves comfortably on earth and to trouble ourselves as little as possible about the crying need in which our brothers in the world find themselves. The “edification” which we here, as God’s people, most need when we gather around his Word at our larger or smaller meetings is this: that we receive more desire and more strength to walk fearlessly on our fixed way, in that life whose wealth and happiness are the joyful self-sacrifice for Jesus Christ’s great, world-liberating task; so that concerning us also the familiar but often forgotten word may become reality, may become truth: “As he is, so are we in this world.”

My meaning, therefore, is that the believers have received true edification during a meeting when they leave the meeting with deeper sorrow over their own powerlessness and wretchedness, with a firmer resolve to cling to grace more childlike and trustfully, and with a more burning longing to be among the ranks of those who have gone out to conquer the world for Christ. The believers are edified when, through the working of the Word, they leave a meeting fully conscious that they, in the one journey they have through life, will follow Jesus Christ.

It will, of course, cost—perhaps much: “And he said to all: If anyone wishes to come after me, he must deny himself and every day take up his cross and follow me. For whoever wishes to save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life for my sake, he will save it.”

And it may indeed happen that the heart will at times writhe in pain over what it will cost. But nevertheless—the same heart that found salvation in Jesus says to him in full earnest:

“Yours, O Jesus, yours to be,
is my joy and all my honor,
yours in life and yours in death.
Therefore I will struggle onward,
in your footsteps I will tread,
find the hour of battle sweet.

Lead me where death itself threatens,
lead me through affliction’s flames,
through the storm upon the wild sea;
as you will, O dearest Jesus,
if only you, only you, will be
yourself my strong supporting staff!”

[Page 243]

And while we, according to the grace we receive, fight the fight against ourselves and our own wretchedness and against the evil in the world around us, our heart is lifted up in blessed joy at possessing God’s unchangeable love in Christ, and we count everything as loss, if only we may keep it. And he himself has taught us that when the battle has been fought to its end and the way of the cross in following him here in this foreign land has been traversed, we will then accompany him into the land of perfection and there be together with him through the long, joy-bringing “Sabbath rest that remains for God’s people.”

“Where I am, there my servant will also be.”
