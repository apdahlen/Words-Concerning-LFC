STEP 1: HAZARD IDENTIFICATION

LEXICAL HAZARDS

* “Forbøn” means intercessory prayer: prayer offered for a meeting, congregation, or other persons. It must not be reduced to prayer in a general sense.

* “Himmelstormende” is literally “heaven-storming.” It describes prayer of extraordinary earnestness and importunity, not loudness or emotional display.

* “Paagaaenhet” denotes persistence, importunity, and determined pressing of a request. “Boldness” alone would not convey its sustained force.

* “Nød” can mean distress, need, affliction, or spiritual extremity. Here it frequently indicates an urgent spiritual condition that drives the soul to prayer.

* “Hvad det gjælder” means “what is at stake,” not merely “what the matter concerns.”

* “Syndens gruelige hærjing” presents sin as a power that ravages or lays waste. The military and destructive force of the expression must remain.

* “Fienden” is the spiritual enemy, ultimately Satan, though the author does not always name him directly.

* “Fællesbøn” and “menighetsbøn” refer to united or congregational prayer. The author distinguishes such prayer from several individuals merely praying in succession.

* “Opbyggelsesmøte” is an edification meeting: a gathering intended for Christian instruction, exhortation, and spiritual upbuilding.

* “Levende erkjendelse” is a living recognition or inwardly active awareness, not mere intellectual acknowledgment.

* “Som ett hjerte, én sjæl” indicates spiritual unity of desire and purpose. It also carries a biblical echo.

* “Samdrægtig” means with one accord, harmoniously, or unitedly. It describes more than physical presence in the same room.

* “Maalbevist” means purposeful, directed toward a definite goal, or governed by a clear intention.

* “Bønhøre” means to hear prayer in the sense of answering it.

* “Sjælens kamp med ham” denotes the soul’s wrestling with God in prayer. The language is deliberately intense and recalls Jacob’s wrestling.

* “Trang” means an inward urge, constraint, longing, or felt necessity. In this context it is the impulse toward prayer produced by the Spirit.

* “Fri, ufortjent naade” must remain “free, unmerited grace.” Neither adjective is redundant in the author’s theological logic.

* “Igrunden” means at bottom, fundamentally, or in reality.

* “Aandelig avmagt” is spiritual powerlessness or impotence. It is stronger than ordinary weakness.

* “Klemt av denne nød” describes a heart pressed or constricted by distress. It must retain the physical pressure of the metaphor.

* “Det samlede sind” is the gathered, concentrated, or wholly directed mind. It is paired with “the undivided heart.”

* “Delagtiggjøres” means to be made a participant in something or to partake of it.

* “Vidnesbyrd” is testimony or witness given publicly in a Christian meeting. It can include doctrinal exposition and exhortation, not only personal autobiography.

* “At sætte sig ind i” means to study, enter into, or become thoroughly acquainted with a subject.

* “Pointeret” means pointed, definite, and sharply articulated.

* “Frelsesveien” is the way of salvation. It has a definite doctrinal meaning and must not be generalized as spiritual growth.

* “Religiøse talemaatter” means familiar religious phrases or formulas whose repetition may conceal a lack of definite thought.

* “At avlægge vidnesbyrd” means to bear or give testimony.

* “Gjængse” means commonly repeated, current, or conventional.

* “Nogen rede paa” means some real understanding or grasp of a matter.

* “Opvarte med” is ironic in this context: to serve up or present something as though it were an acceptable offering.

* “Letsindig” means careless, light-minded, or frivolously irresponsible. It carries moral blame.

* “Hellig krig” denotes spiritual warfare against evil. It must not be interpreted as physical religious violence.

* “Ondskapens aandehær” depicts evil spirits as an organized spiritual host or army.

* “Krigstjeneste” means military service. It presents Christian life as sustained service under discipline and command.

* “Menige” means rank-and-file soldiers.

* “Anførere” means leaders or commanders.

* “Trænge ind i” means to penetrate deeply into the meaning of the biblical text or subject, not merely to glance over it.

SYNTACTIC HAZARDS

* The opening argument proceeds through long, accumulating clauses. Breaking these into numerous short sentences would weaken the movement from deficient prayer to the spiritual battle that makes such prayer necessary.

* The author frequently uses conditional constructions beginning with “om”: if believers saw the danger, if they remembered Christ’s promise, if they grasped spiritual need. These conditions build moral accountability.

* Parenthetical clauses often intensify rather than interrupt the argument. The description of the meeting as a battlefield is essential to the reason intercession should become a cry of distress.

* The dialogue “Ja, jo, siger nogen” introduces an imagined objection. Its conversational quality should remain without becoming casual.

* The passage contrasts apparent common prayer with actual common prayer through repeated qualifications: “It may be this, but it may also be only that.” This distinction must not be compressed.

* Several sentences employ resumptive syntax, repeating “that” or returning to the main clause after a long subordinate clause. This reflects older Scandinavian sermonic prose and should be retained where intelligible.

* Biblical quotations are embedded as authoritative supports within the argument. They should remain structurally distinct and should not be converted into indirect speech.

* The paragraph beginning with the assertion that God works both willing and doing contains a carefully balanced theological paradox. Its clauses must remain connected.

* The question concerning why prayer can be significant leads immediately to a doctrinal answer. The question-and-answer form must remain visible.

* The repeated forms “The one who...” and “Those who...” establish a series of obligations. Variation for stylistic elegance would weaken the didactic structure.

* The transition from unprepared speaking to the soldier analogy is deliberate and cumulative. It should not be treated as a decorative illustration detached from the argument.

* The quotation from Ephesians consists of a sequence of coordinated imperatives. Its accumulating military rhythm must be preserved.

* The sentence “Og saa vil beskylde Paulus...” appears to omit an indefinite subject, probably “man.” The intended sense is most plausibly: “And then people will accuse Paul of not understanding what Christianity is.” The translation supplies the implied subject without altering the argument.

THEOLOGICAL HAZARDS

* Prayer is not presented as a human work that earns blessing. It is the Spirit-produced willingness to receive help given in free and unmerited grace.

* The author nevertheless insists upon praying as earnestly as though the blessing depended wholly upon prayer. This apparent tension must not be resolved or softened.

* Failure to pray is interpreted as evidence that the soul does not truly perceive spiritual danger. The author’s conclusion is diagnostic and severe.

* Salvation from sin and spiritual powerlessness is described as so great that it can be received only by an undivided heart pressed by its need. This must not be reduced to psychological sincerity.

* The prayer of united believers is connected with Christ’s promise and God’s answer, not with collective emotional enthusiasm.

* Christ’s nail-pierced hands unite the crucifixion, the promise of drawing humanity, and the church’s intercession. The image must remain explicitly Christological.

* “God’s Word” denotes revealed divine truth and not merely religious discourse.

* The way of salvation includes concrete truths about sin, guilt before God, and salvation through Jesus Christ. These doctrinal particulars must not be generalized.

* “The life in God” refers to the actual Christian life that God’s child is obligated to live on earth.

* The language of warfare is entirely spiritual: the enemy is the prince of darkness and the spiritual host of wickedness.

* Preachers bear heightened responsibility because they are compared not merely to soldiers but to leaders and officers in Christ’s army.

VOICE HAZARDS

* Modernizing the prose into concise institutional language would erase its pastoral urgency.

* Rendering “himmelstormende” merely as “fervent” would weaken the extremity of the author’s complaint.

* The repeated first-person plural implicates the author and readers together. It should not be replaced with detached references to “people” or “churches.”

* Statements such as “then we shall never receive it” must remain absolute and alarming.

* The author’s criticisms of boring, aimless preaching are intentionally direct. Euphemisms such as “less engaging” would distort his judgment.

* “Careless and irresponsible” must retain its moral force.

* The military imagery should remain solemn rather than theatrical.

* The ironic sentence about accusing Paul must retain its sharpness.

* Consolation appears chiefly in God’s promises, Christ’s outstretched hands, and free grace. It should not be spread evenly across the severe passages.

* The prose is earnest rather than sentimental. Emotional force arises from spiritual consequence and doctrinal logic.

STEP 2: IDIOM ANALYSIS

* “Himmelstormende” is an established devotional image of prayer pressing urgently toward heaven. A flat rendering such as “very sincere” would lose the image and the sense of importunity. “Heaven-storming” preserves both.

* “Vor sjæl er truffet av ... nød” pictures distress as something that strikes the soul. The author is not merely saying that a person becomes concerned.

* “Hvad det gjælder” means “what is at stake.” It introduces the realities that should govern prayer: sin’s devastation and the enemy’s advance.

* “En slagmark, hvor mørkets og lysets magter møtes til kamp” is spiritual warfare imagery. The meeting itself is understood as a place where truth and evil contend for human beings.

* “Et nødrop til Herren” is a cry arising from extremity. “Request” or “petition” would be too mild.

* “Som ett hjerte, én sjæl” recalls the unity of the first Christians, especially Acts 4:32, where the believers are described as being of one heart and soul.

* “Blir enige om at bede” comes from Matthew 18:19. The agreement is not merely verbal consensus but united petition.

* “Holdt samdrægtig ved i bønnen” echoes Acts 1:14. The King James expression “continued with one accord in prayer” closely reflects the older biblical cadence. The final translation retains “with one accord” while using modern pronouns elsewhere.

* “Naglegjennemstukne hænder” refers to the wounds of the crucified Christ. It also evokes the risen Christ’s displayed wounds and the enduring efficacy of his sacrifice.

* “Naar jeg blir ophøiet fra jorden, vil jeg dra alle til mig” is John 12:32. “Lifted up” refers simultaneously to crucifixion and exaltation.

* “Det er Gud, som virker i os baade at ville og at utrette” is Philippians 2:13. The traditional English form “to will and to do” is natural, but “to accomplish” better reflects the wording quoted here without introducing archaic diction.

* “Jeg slipper dig ikke, før du velsigner mig” is Jacob’s declaration in Genesis 32:26. The King James wording is highly recognizable, but the final translation uses modern pronouns: “I will not let you go until you bless me.”

* “Hvem har jeg i himlene?” echoes Psalm 73:25. The following line expresses exclusive desire for God.

* “Det samlede sind, det udelte hjerte” describes a person whose desires have ceased to be divided among competing goods.

* “Aand og liv” is from John 6:63. The phrase signifies that Christ’s words are not dead religious material.

* “Den, som taler bør tale som Guds ord” echoes 1 Peter 4:11. The sense is not that the speaker’s personal opinions become Scripture, but that one who speaks in the church must speak in accordance with God’s revealed words.

* “Gamle, tilvante ... religiøse talemaatter” refers to stock religious expressions repeated without coherent engagement with the appointed text.

* The soldier who goes to battle without inspecting his weapons is a culturally familiar military analogy. It establishes preparation as duty rather than optional refinement.

* “Hellig krig” means holy spiritual warfare. A merely literal rendering could suggest physical conflict if isolated, but the next sentence explicitly identifies the enemies as the prince of darkness and spiritual wickedness.

* “Ondskapens aandehær under himmelen” echoes Ephesians 6:12. The King James Bible has “spiritual wickedness in high places,” while many modern versions use “spiritual forces of evil in the heavenly places.” The author’s martial noun “host” or “army” should remain visible.

* “Omgjørdet om eders lend med sandhet” is the biblical image of gathering one’s garments with a belt so that one is ready for action.

* “Troens skjold,” “frelsens hjelm,” and “Aandens sverd” are not explanatory abstractions but a coordinated set of military metaphors. Each must remain intact.

* “Kristi seirende hær” presents the church as Christ’s already victorious army, even while its earthly conflict continues.

STEP 3: ARGUMENT STRUCTURE IDENTIFICATION

* The author begins by adding a second observation to the argument of his preceding article.

* His first claim is that intercession for Christian meetings is often powerless because it lacks intensity, persistence, and heartfelt desire.

* He contrasts this weak intercession with the way people pray when personally struck by severe distress.

* He then identifies the cause: believers do not see clearly enough what is at stake in the spiritual conflict.

* A meeting is defined as a battlefield where the powers of light and darkness contend.

* The author next introduces a more specific deficiency: united congregational prayer is scarcely practiced.

* He anticipates an objection that several persons do pray at prayer meetings.

* He distinguishes several individual prayers from genuine united prayer.

* Genuine united prayer requires a congregation to recognize a particular need, desire one thing as one heart and soul, and ask God for it together.

* Matthew 18:19–20 is introduced as Christ’s promise concerning agreed prayer and his presence among the gathered believers.

* Acts 1:13–14 is introduced as an example of sustained prayer with one accord before Pentecost.

* From these texts the author concludes that true united prayer brings help and blessing from God.

* He asserts that more prayer of this kind before meetings would bring greater blessing.

* Christ’s nail-pierced hands and his promise to draw all to himself should deepen the seriousness of intercession.

* The first major conclusion is severe: prayer is either absent or so lacking in definite purpose that an answer can scarcely be expected.

* The author then states the apparent theological objection: God himself works both the will and the action, and even the soul’s wrestling prayer is his work.

* He nevertheless insists that believers must pray as though the blessing depended entirely upon their prayer.

* He raises the question of why prayer matters when prayer itself is produced by the Spirit.

* His answer is that prayer signifies willingness to receive the help and blessing God freely desires to give.

* Lack of earnest prayer shows that a person does not truly see the danger and does not truly care about deliverance.

* Salvation from sin and spiritual powerlessness can be received only by an undivided heart overwhelmed by its need.

* Psalm 73 supplies the language of exclusive desire for God.

* The author restates his practical purpose: to expose the lack of united congregational prayer before larger meetings and to awaken serious consideration of the matter.

* He then introduces a second common deficiency: speakers often speak vaguely, unclearly, and without a definite aim.

* The chief cause is lack of preparation in the biblical text or subject.

* The consequence is either departure from the text or vague general speech that bores the hearers.

* This is judged especially unacceptable because the Word being read is spirit and life.

* God’s Word is defined as concrete truth concerning sin, guilt, salvation through Jesus Christ, and the life God’s child must live.

* Therefore testimony by either layman or pastor must consist of definite truths drawn from the appointed text or subject.

* Lack of time is rejected as an excuse. A person without time to prepare should not speak.

* The importance of the subject should itself compel the best possible preparation.

* Hearers also have a duty to prepare through prayer and reflection upon the Word.

* Speakers should possess at least a real grasp of the text and should be able to state its truths with reasonable clarity.

* The hearers have a right to expect this.

* The age of unrelated stock religious phrases ought to be past.

* The meager fruit of such speech corresponds to its deficient character.

* Going to speak without preparation is therefore careless and irresponsible.

* The soldier who enters battle without inspecting or learning to use his weapons illustrates the unprepared speaker.

* Giving testimony is identified as entering holy spiritual warfare.

* Ephesians 6 establishes that every Christian’s life is military service.

* The author then uses irony: after Paul’s plainly martial description, people may still accuse him of misunderstanding Christianity.

* What applies to Christians generally applies still more to preachers.

* Preachers are not merely ordinary soldiers but should be officers in Christ’s victorious army.

* Failure is unsurprising where armor and weapons are absent or unused.

* The final appeal requires serious preparation through heartfelt prayer and conscientious penetration into the biblical text or subject.

STEP 4: BIBLICAL ECHO IDENTIFICATION

* Matthew 18:19–20: agreement in prayer, the Father’s answer, and Christ’s presence where two or three are gathered in his name.

* Acts 1:13–14: the apostles, women, Mary, and Jesus’ brothers continuing with one accord in prayer before Pentecost.

* Acts 4:32: the believers being of one heart and one soul. This is probably echoed in the author’s definition of united congregational prayer.

* John 12:32: Christ being lifted up from the earth and drawing all to himself.

* John 20:20, 25–27: the nail wounds of the risen Christ may contribute to the image of his nail-pierced hands.

* Philippians 2:13: God working in believers both to will and to accomplish.

* Genesis 32:26: Jacob refusing to let go until he receives a blessing.

* Psalm 73:25: “Whom have I in heaven?” and the rejection of every earthly desire in comparison with God.

* John 6:63: Christ’s words being spirit and life.

* 1 Peter 4:11: the one who speaks doing so as one who speaks words of God.

* Ephesians 6:12: conflict against spiritual powers or the spiritual host of wickedness.

* Ephesians 6:14–17: the belt of truth, breastplate of righteousness, readiness of the gospel, shield of faith, helmet of salvation, and sword of the Spirit.

* 2 Corinthians 6:14 and Colossians 1:13 may stand behind the contrast between the powers of light and darkness, though no direct quotation is made.

* The general description of Christian life as military service also resembles 2 Timothy 2:3–4.

STEP 5: VOICE INTENSITY PRESERVATION

* The translation retains “powerless,” “heaven-storming,” “dreadful ravaging,” “threatening advance,” and “cry of distress” rather than substituting milder devotional language.

* The diagnosis that believers do not truly see the danger remains direct.

* The conclusion “Then we shall never receive it” is retained without qualification.

* The hearers’ boredom is stated plainly rather than being reframed as a problem of engagement.

* Speaking without preparation remains “careless and irresponsible.”

* The preacher’s obligation is presented as a matter of spiritual warfare and command responsibility.

* Divine grace is expressed warmly, but it does not cancel the severity of the author’s rebuke.

* The author’s use of “we” and “our” is preserved so that the argument continues to address the conscience of both writer and reader.

STEP 6: MODAL FORCE PRESERVATION

* “Skal” is translated as “shall” where it expresses a divine promise or assured result.

* “Vil” is translated according to context as “will,” “wills,” or “desires,” without reducing it to mere possibility.

* “Maa” does not occur prominently in the supplied passage, but equivalent necessities are preserved through “must” or firm constructions where required.

* “Bør” is translated as “should” or “ought,” retaining moral obligation rather than offering a suggestion.

* “Kan” is not automatically translated as permission. Where it expresses actual capacity, “can” or “is able” is used.

* “Kan neppe ventes” remains “can scarcely be expected,” preserving the author’s severe judgment.

* “Bør ikke delta” remains “should not take part,” not “might prefer not to.”

* “Har ret til at vente” remains “have a right to expect.”

* “Ingen ... har lov til” becomes “no one has the right,” preserving prohibition.

* The divine promise in Matthew 18 remains “it shall be given,” rather than “it may be given.”

STEP 7: CONSISTENT THEOLOGICAL TERM RENDERING

* “Gud” is consistently rendered “God.”

* “Herren” is consistently rendered “the Lord.”

* “Aanden” is rendered “the Spirit” when referring to the Holy Spirit.

* “Guds ord” is rendered “God’s Word.”

* “Synd” is rendered “sin.”

* “Skyld” is rendered “guilt.”

* “Naade” is rendered “grace.”

* “Ufortjent naade” is rendered “unmerited grace.”

* “Forbøn” is rendered “intercession” or “intercessory prayer.”

* “Fællesbøn” is rendered “united prayer.”

* “Menighetsbøn” is rendered “congregational prayer.”

* “Frelse” is normally rendered “salvation.” “Deliverance” is used only where the immediate emphasis is rescue in distress, while the doctrinal phrase “salvation from sin” remains stable.

* “Aandelig nød” is rendered “spiritual distress.”

* “Aandelig avmagt” is rendered “spiritual powerlessness.”

* “Liv i Gud” is rendered “life in God.”

* “Vidnesbyrd” is rendered “testimony,” with verbal forms such as “give testimony” or “bear testimony.”

* “Frelsesveien” is rendered “the way of salvation.”

* “Mørkhetens fyrste” is rendered “the prince of darkness.”

STEP 8: METAPHOR INTEGRITY PRESERVATION

* Prayer continues to “storm heaven.”

* Distress continues to strike the soul.

* Sin continues to ravage the world.

* The enemy continues to advance.

* The meeting remains a battlefield.

* Intercession becomes a cry of distress.

* Christ stretches out nail-pierced hands.

* The soul wrestles with God and refuses to release him without a blessing.

* Spiritual distress presses the heart.

* Salvation becomes an overwhelming reality in which the heart partakes.

* God’s Word remains spirit and life.

* The biblical text remains a reality into which the speaker must penetrate.

* The unprepared speaker remains a soldier who has neither inspected nor practiced with his weapons.

* Testimony remains participation in holy warfare.

STEP 9: TONE CONTRAST PRESERVATION

* The opening severity concerning powerless prayer gives way to the promise of Christ’s presence and the apostolic example of united prayer.

* The assurance that God works prayer within believers is held together with the demand that they pray with utmost earnestness.

* The warmth of free, unmerited grace is followed by the warning that an indifferent heart will never receive the salvation it does not desire.

* The Psalm quotation introduces concentrated longing for God after the severe diagnosis of spiritual indifference.

* The author’s personal gladness at possible reform briefly softens the passage before the next rebuke begins.

* The assertion that God’s Word is spirit and life intensifies the condemnation of vague and boring speech.

* The martial conclusion combines confidence in Christ’s victorious army with a severe warning concerning absent armor and unused weapons.

STEP 10: ORAL CADENCE AWARENESS

* Long sentences are retained where the author accumulates causes and consequences.

* Semicolons and dashes preserve rhetorical pivots without fragmenting the reasoning.

* Repetition of “For,” “But,” “Therefore,” and “And” retains the explicit connective logic of oral proclamation.

* Scriptural quotations are allowed to carry their own cadence.

* Questions are followed by direct doctrinal answers, preserving the rhythm of catechetical exposition.

* Parallel constructions remain parallel.

* Repeated obligations beginning with “The one who...” retain their admonitory pattern.

* The Ephesians quotation is presented as a continuous succession of commands, producing the rhythm of arming for battle.

* The final sentence gathers prayer, conscience, study, the Word, and the meeting into one concluding obligation.

STEP 11: ACADEMIC TRANSLATION

[Page 274]

Some Words on the Lutheran Free Church and Its Tasks

E. B.

[ornament]

V.

Besides what I pointed out in my previous article, another matter may also be mentioned in connection with intercessory prayer for a meeting: our prayer, and perhaps especially our intercession, is often so powerless, so little heaven-storming and heartfelt.

So often there is lacking that intensity and persistence with which we pray for ourselves when our soul has been struck by some grievous distress. Then we pray because we want something, because we desire something from God.

And if we were clear-sighted enough to see what is at stake—the dreadful ravaging of sin in the world, the threatening advance of the enemy—then intercession for our meetings, which we expect to be a battlefield where the powers of darkness and light meet in combat, would also become far more a cry of distress to the Lord and would bring victory from him.

Yet another matter may also be pointed out in this connection: united prayer, congregational prayer. It is scarcely ever practiced among us. Yes, someone says, when we are gathered for a prayer meeting, several of us pray for blessing upon the approaching edification meeting or mission meeting.

Now, yes, that is well and good where it is done. For not even this is practiced everywhere. But there may nevertheless be little true united prayer in it. It may be such, but it may also be only individual prayer, the prayer of single persons, and nothing more. For in the prayer for “blessing upon the meeting,” one person may have one thing in mind and another something entirely different. By united prayer for a meeting I mean particularly this: that a congregation has a living recognition of what it especially needs to receive from the Lord, and that it therefore, as one heart and one soul, longs to obtain this and therefore asks it of him in prayer.

“Again I say to you: Whatever two of you on earth agree to ask for, it shall be given them by my Father in heaven. For where two or three are gathered in my name, there I am in the midst of them.”

And concerning the apostles before the day of Pentecost it is said:

“They went up into the upper room, where they were staying—Peter and John and James and Andrew, Philip and Thomas, Bartholomew and Matthew, James the son of Alphaeus, and Simon the Zealot, and Judas the son of James. All these continued with one accord in prayer, together with some women and Mary, the mother of Jesus, and his brothers.”

Here we have true united prayer, the prayer that brings blessing, help from God. And for my part I am certain that if more prayer of this kind were practiced before our meetings, these would prove a greater blessing than they now often are.

Could we only remember vividly that the same Lord who has promised to hear prayer when two agree to pray for victory from God over evil—that he stretches out his nail-pierced hands toward human beings and says, “When I am lifted up from the earth, I will draw all to myself”—there would surely be more seriousness in our intercession. The conclusion, then, at which I arrive in the question of preparing our meetings by prayer is this: either no prayer at all is offered, or, if it is offered by one person or another, it is done with so little definite purpose that an answer from God to the prayer can scarcely be expected.

[Page 275]

Certainly it is true that “it is God who works in us both to will and to accomplish,” so that our most heartfelt prayer, and that wrestling of the soul with him in which it is said, “I will not let you go until you bless me,” are also his work. Yet, in spite of this, he wills that we should pray with such earnestness as though the blessing of a meeting depended exclusively upon our prayer. But why can prayer be so significant when it too is his own impulse and work in us, brought forth by the Spirit?

My answer to this question is that our prayer means nothing other than that we are willing to receive the help and the blessing which the Lord, in free, unmerited grace, so gladly desires to give us, and which we so sorely need. If our soul has not been so deeply struck by our own and others’ spiritual distress that we ask him with full earnestness for deliverance in that distress, then this is the clearest sign that at bottom we see no danger and therefore do not care about deliverance from God in the distress. Then we shall never receive it. For salvation from sin and spiritual powerlessness is so great that it can be received only by a heart so pressed by this distress that, in comparison with it, it desires nothing else:

“Whom have I in heaven? And when I have you, I desire nothing on earth!”

In this cry we encounter the gathered mind, the undivided heart, for which salvation has become an overwhelmingly great reality, and which therefore partakes of it in so rich a measure.

What I intended by the little I have said here was, then, this: to point out a deficiency which, in my judgment, is so common at our larger meetings, namely, that as a rule they are far too little prepared for by united prayer, congregational prayer. And could I bring individual believers and our congregations to begin thinking over and considering this matter and, if possible, to take it more seriously, I should be deeply glad.

Another very common deficiency at most of our larger meetings is that those who are to take part by giving testimony often speak vaguely, unclearly, and without any aim.

And one of the most common causes of this is that the person concerned is unprepared. That is to say: the person has not studied the Word of God or the subject that is to be treated during the meeting.

But the natural consequence is that in such a case the speaker either does not speak on the text at all, or, if he keeps within the framework of the text, speaks so generally, so vaguely, and with so little point that the hearers are simply bored. And this ought not to be so when the word that is read is “spirit and life.” The Bible says: “Whoever speaks should speak as one who speaks words of God.” And God’s Word concerning the way of salvation is not religious phrases, but clear, definite, concrete truths about sin and guilt before God and about salvation from sin through Jesus Christ. And God’s Word also contains clear, definite truths concerning the life in God which his child is to live here on earth.

Therefore the testimony borne by layman or pastor during a meeting should consist of definite truths drawn from the subject or passage of Scripture that is being treated. No one has the right to excuse himself here with the often-repeated claim that one “has not had time to prepare.” For the one who has had no time for any preparation should not take part by giving testimony. If the matter concerning which we gather for a meeting is not so important that it drives us to as good and thorough a preparation as possible, it is better not to take up any of the meeting’s brief, precious time as a speaker. But not only the one who is to speak at a meeting should prepare himself. The one who is to hear should do the same: prepare himself through prayer and through consideration of the Word of God that is to be treated. By this means the benefit to the hearers would in every case be greater than it otherwise is.

The one who is to speak at a meeting should at least have some grasp of what the word on which he is to speak contains, and he should be able to set forth with reasonable clarity truths contained in the subject before him. Those who come to the meeting to hear have a right to expect this, and many of them really do expect it.

The time should at least be past when, at meetings such as those intended here, one can serve up old, familiar, assorted religious phrases without coherence and without any connection whatever with the subject of the meeting. Certainly this can still be done, and unfortunately it is probably done far too often; but the effects, the fruit, correspond to it. It is therefore careless and irresponsible when someone travels to a meeting to take part by giving testimony without having made any attempt to prepare himself. What would one say of the soldier who went into battle without having inspected his weapons and without having practiced using them? He would at least be able to accomplish little for the king or general in whose army he was to fight against the enemy.

And to take part by giving testimony at a meeting is to enter a holy war. It is to enter war against the prince of darkness and “the spiritual host of wickedness under heaven.”

Paul presents every Christian’s life here on earth as military service. He says:

“Stand, then, with truth girded about your loins and clothed with the breastplate of righteousness, and with your feet shod with the readiness that the gospel of salvation gives; and besides all this, take up the shield of faith, with which you shall be able to quench all the burning arrows of the evil one, and take the helmet of salvation and the sword of the Spirit, which is God’s Word.”

And then people will accuse Paul of not understanding what Christianity is.

But if this applies to Christians in general, then it applies to preachers in particular. For such men are not merely rank-and-file soldiers; they should be leaders, officers in Christ’s victorious army on earth. And where the armor and weapons are lacking or are not used, we need not wonder at the unsuccessful result of the battle.

None of us, therefore, should go to a meeting with the thought of accomplishing something for our Lord’s great cause without serious preparation through heartfelt prayer and a conscientious effort to penetrate the Word of God or the subject that is to be treated during the meeting.
