STEP 1: HAZARD IDENTIFICATION

Lexical Hazards

* “Spørgsmaalet om Gud” is not merely “the question of God” in an abstract philosophical sense. In context it means the deeply personal question concerning God—whether God exists in relation to the human soul and, above all, whether he cares for the individual.
* “rives ut av menneskesjælen” literally means “torn out of the human soul.” The violence of the image should be preserved; “removed” would be too weak.
* “utgaat fra Gud” means “come forth from God” or “proceeded from God.” It refers to the soul’s origin in God as Creator, not to an ontological emanation from the divine essence.
* “trænges tilbake” means “pressed back” or “pushed aside,” not permanently eradicated.
* “faa greie paa” means to gain knowledge or certainty concerning something. A colloquial modern rendering such as “figure out” would weaken the solemn register.
* “hjertelag” is especially important. It means disposition of heart, compassionate feeling, or loving concern toward another. Here the question is whether God has a heart toward us—whether his inward disposition toward helpless human beings is one of love and compassion.
* “hjælpeløse menneskebarn” literally “helpless children of men/human children.” The expression carries devotional vulnerability and should not be flattened to simply “people.”
* “trakter til at læse” means “happens/chances to read.” The phrase belongs to an older conversational sermonic idiom.
* “attraar” is an older, elevated term meaning “longs for,” “yearns for,” or “desires ardently.”
* “visheten” means “the certainty” or “assurance,” not merely intellectual knowledge.
* “ussel” can mean wretched, miserable, paltry, or contemptible. In this penitential context “wretched” preserves the moral and spiritual self-abasement better than “poor.”
* “livsspørgsmaalet” is literally “the life-question”: the decisive question upon which one’s life turns. “Fundamental question of life” is preferable to a casual modern paraphrase.
* “paa bunden av menneskehjertet” means “at the bottom/depth of the human heart.” It indicates the deepest level of human spiritual concern.
* “salig oplevelse” combines blessedness and experiential religious assurance. “Blessed experience” should retain its pietistic resonance.
* “vet sig elsket” means “knows oneself to be loved,” a reflexive construction expressing conscious assurance.
* “benaadet” is doctrinally weighty: “shown grace,” “received into grace,” or “pardoned by grace.” “Forgiven” alone may be too narrow because the term points to being an object of divine grace.
* “selvtagen indbildning” means a self-assumed or self-generated delusion/imagination—an assurance one has taken to oneself without divine warrant.
* “trygt” means secure, dependable, safe to entrust oneself to.
* “leve paa det — dø paa det” means something upon which one can stake both life and death. The repetition and parallelism should be retained.
* “forklaret ham” in the biblical quotation has the older biblical sense “made him known,” “declared him,” or “revealed him,” rather than merely “explained him.”
* “fortrolige samkjendskap” is unusual and intimate language: confidential/intimate communion and shared knowledge. It expresses the Son’s unique inward knowledge of the Father.
* “Elsk er Gud” appears to be a compressed or typographically unusual form of “God is love.” It must be understood in connection with 1 John 4:8, 16 and Jesus’ testimony to the Father’s love.
* “Jeg er hos ham” means more than geographical proximity; in context it expresses belonging and filial nearness to the Father.
* “gaar paa egne veier” is the biblical/devotional idiom “goes his own ways,” implying estrangement and self-will.
* “indremission” refers historically to “Home Mission,” missionary/evangelistic work directed toward one’s own nominally Christian population rather than foreign mission.
* “nysettlementer” is Norwegian-American usage: new settlements, particularly immigrant settlements.
* “dit til” means “your part toward it,” not merely a vague “something.”
* “angrende og bøiet” means repentant and bowed/humbled before God.
* “fuld tilgivelse” means full forgiveness, with emphasis on completeness.
* “saaret av den pil som kom fra fienden” is spiritual warfare imagery. “The enemy” is implicitly Satan, and “arrow” likely evokes the fiery darts of the wicked one.
* “sviende saar” means smarting/burning wounds, retaining bodily immediacy as a metaphor for wounded conscience.
* “legemlig nød” means bodily/physical distress or need.
* “sjælenød” is a strongly pietistic term: anguish/distress of soul, especially under conviction of sin.
* “angst og bæven” means “fear/anxiety and trembling,” with biblical resonance.
* “faderhjertet” means “the Father-heart,” a characteristic devotional compound referring to God’s paternal love and compassion. Simply “God’s love” would erase the metaphor.
* “forbarmende” means compassionate, merciful, moved with pity.
* “utslettet” is “blotted out,” a biblical term for the removal of sin.
* “fuldtonende” literally means “full-toned,” suggesting that the proclamation should sound out clearly, strongly, and without diminution.
* “grundfaste klippe” means the firmly founded, immovable rock; it is a traditional biblical image of secure faith.

Syntactic Hazards

* The opening proceeds by accumulated reflective clauses rather than short declarative propositions. The translation should retain that movement from the universal “question of God” to the specific question of divine love.
* Several paragraphs employ rhetorical questions whose answers are deliberately delayed. These should remain questions rather than being converted into explanatory statements.
* The sequence “om han bryr sig om os, om han har hjertelag overfor os” is parallel and cumulative. Both members should be preserved.
* “Intet kan helt tilfredsstille dig av alt det du opnaadde...” is syntactically older and slightly awkward by modern standards. The English should preserve the underlying claim without making it excessively colloquial.
* “Er jeg, saa liten og ussel, saa ond og syndig jeg er,— er jeg elsket av Gud?” uses interruption and repetition to enact the troubled conscience. The dash and renewed question should be retained.
* “leve paa det — dø paa det” is a climactic parallel structure; it should not be smoothed into a single phrase.
* The twofold “Hans svar er dette” is deliberate repetition and should remain.
* “elsker os — elsker, elsker saa at han gav sin søn” intensifies through repetition. English must not remove this apparent redundancy.
* “ikke engang da, naar barnet glemmer ham — gaar paa egne veier og faar det ondt” unfolds as a parenthetical pastoral picture and should retain the dash-driven cadence.
* The direct appeal beginning “Du som træffer til at læse dette...” changes from theological exposition to personal summons. That pivot must remain conspicuous.
* The long paragraph beginning “Der gjøres i denne tid...” is structured by comparison: bodily suffering is acknowledged as worthy of mercy, then spiritual anguish is declared still more grave, then remembered personal experience becomes the basis for missionary obligation. It should not be broken into disconnected modern paragraphs.
* The repeated “Og det vet du...” presses experiential testimony upon the reader. Its anaphoric force should be retained.
* “Da blev du glad midt i sorgen, rik midt i fattigdommen, lykkelig i den haardeste motgang” is a threefold antithetical parallelism and should remain balanced.
* The final missionary appeal moves through command, historical urgency, hymn quotation, and direct question. Its oral progression must remain intact.

Theological Hazards

* The central theological issue is not a general proof of God but assurance of God’s gracious disposition toward sinners.
* Human dissatisfaction with created goods is presented as a spiritual longing ultimately directed toward God and specifically toward the assurance of being loved by God.
* The sinner’s question “Am I loved by God?” is answered not through subjective religious experience but through the authoritative revelation given in Christ.
* Human testimony to grace is valuable but insufficient as the foundation of certainty because human beings may mistake both God and themselves.
* Christ’s unique authority rests upon his unique knowledge of the Father as the only-begotten Son.
* John 1:18 is used Christologically: the Son makes the Father known.
* John 3:16 provides the objective ground for assurance: God’s giving of the Son.
* Luke 15:7 provides the heavenly confirmation of divine concern for the repentant sinner.
* Repentance is represented by the prodigal’s confession, “Father, I have sinned,” and is linked with assurance of complete forgiveness through the gospel.
* “God is my Father” must not be detached from Christ and the gospel; it is not presented as generic universal spirituality.
* The child who wanders and suffers evokes the prodigal son and divine paternal compassion.
* The gospel is explicitly defined as the message of salvation from sin and death through Jesus Christ.
* Spiritual wounds are attributed to “the enemy” and healed uniquely through the gospel.
* Spiritual distress over sin is treated as more grave than bodily distress. This hierarchical theological claim must not be softened merely because modern discourse might express it differently.
* “grace, mercy, salvation” form a doctrinal triad and should remain distinct.
* Forgiveness is described with several complementary terms: forgiven, sin blotted out, sin remitted/left behind. The cumulative absolution imagery should be preserved.
* The mission appeal follows from received grace: those who know the gospel’s consolation are morally implicated in bringing it to others.

Voice Hazards

* The author alternates between reflective exposition and direct second-person address. English should preserve this personal penetration rather than becoming academic or detached.
* The text speaks directly to conscience: “You ... know that this is so.” Such assertions should not be weakened into “perhaps you may feel.”
* “O”/“Aa” expresses earnest devotional emotion. Excessive modernization into casual exclamation would be inappropriate.
* Repetition—especially “loves, loves”—carries spiritual intensity and should not automatically be edited away.
* The author’s missionary appeal is urgent but not promotional. Modern fundraising diction should be avoided.
* The Red Cross comparison is respectful and deliberately concessive: bodily relief is “a blessed work,” but spiritual need is greater. Both elements must be retained to preserve the argument’s ethical seriousness.
* The final “Will you?” is intentionally simple and searching. It should not be expanded into a modern inspirational conclusion.

STEP 2: IDIOM ANALYSIS

* “Spørgsmaalet om Gud kan ikke godt rives ut av menneskesjælen.”

  * Literally, the question concerning God cannot easily be torn out of the human soul.
  * The image presents God-consciousness as something deeply rooted in human nature.
  * “Torn out” should be retained because it suggests violent suppression rather than simple intellectual neglect.

* “utgaat fra Gud”

  * Means that the soul has its origin from God because God created it.
  * It must not be translated in language suggesting that the soul is an emanation or fragment of God.

* “har hjertelag overfor os”

  * Literally concerns God’s “heart-disposition” toward humanity.
  * The phrase asks whether God has compassion, affection, and gracious concern for us.
  * A literal “has heart toward us” is possible but slightly opaque in English; “has a loving heart toward us” or “has compassion toward us” carries the intended force.

* “Din sjæl attraar noget mere.”

  * “Your soul longs for something more.”
  * The elevated “longs” is preferable to “wants.”

* “vet sig elsket av Gud”

  * “know themselves to be loved by God.”
  * This is experiential assurance rather than speculative belief.

* “leve paa det — dø paa det”

  * A traditional religious formulation concerning an assurance sufficient for both life and death.
  * The meaning is: can this answer bear the whole weight of my existence and remain trustworthy at death?
  * The literal parallelism works naturally in English: “live upon it—die upon it.”

* “Den enbaarne søn som er i Faderens skjød”

  * John 1:18.
  * “In the bosom of the Father” is historically familiar English biblical language, but under the requested profile an unnecessarily inherited KJV coloring should be resisted.
  * “Who is at the Father’s breast” would be too physical and distracting.
  * “Who is in the Father’s bosom” best preserves the image and traditional theological resonance without archaic pronouns.

* “han har forklaret ham”

  * John 1:18.
  * Older Scandinavian biblical “forklaret” means “declared/made known.”
  * “He has made him known” is the clearest modern-pronoun equivalent.

* “fortrolige samkjendskap til sin fader”

  * Refers to Christ’s intimate and unique knowledge of the Father.
  * It is not mere intellectual familiarity.
  * “intimate fellowship and knowledge of his Father” best conveys the compound idea.

* “Saa har Gud elsket verden”

  * John 3:16.
  * The older Scandinavian syntax corresponds to “For God so loved the world.”
  * Since the translation should resist merely importing inherited KJV tone, “God so loved the world that he gave his Son” can retain the structure without archaic diction.

* “glæde i himmelen over én synder som omvender sig”

  * Luke 15:7.
  * “Repents” is doctrinally preferable to “turns around,” since “omvender sig” here denotes repentance/conversion.

* “gaar paa egne veier”

  * Literally “goes on his own ways.”
  * It evokes biblical language of wandering and self-will, especially Isaiah 53:6 and the prodigal son.
  * “goes his own way” preserves the biblical echo naturally.

* “Fader, jeg har syndet!”

  * From the prodigal son’s confession in Luke 15:18, 21.
  * The filial address is crucial: repentance occurs precisely before the Father whose love the surrounding discourse has described.

* “saaret av den pil som kom fra fienden”

  * Most naturally evokes Ephesians 6:16 and the “fiery darts/arrows of the evil one.”
  * “The enemy” is a traditional designation for Satan.
  * The metaphor should remain an arrow and wound, rather than being paraphrased as “spiritual pain.”

* “sjælens sviende saar”

  * Literally “the soul’s smarting wounds.”
  * The image conveys active, burning pain of conscience.
  * “the soul’s burning wounds” gives more vivid English than “painful wounds.”

* “angst og bæven”

  * Likely carries biblical associations with “fear and trembling,” a recurring biblical formula.
  * “Fear and trembling” is preferable to “anxiety and shaking,” because the former preserves the scriptural echo.

* “fik se ind i faderhjertet”

  * Literally “were allowed to look into the Father-heart.”
  * The point is revelatory: through the gospel the sinner perceives the compassion at the heart of God.
  * “look into the Father’s heart” preserves the metaphor and is more idiomatic than “Father-heart.”

* “alt er tilgit, al synd utslettet, forlatt”

  * A cumulative absolution formula.
  * “all is forgiven, all sin blotted out, remitted” preserves distinct images better than repeating “forgiven.”

* “klart og fuldtonende”

  * Literally “clearly and full-toned.”
  * The gospel must sound with unweakened fullness.
  * “clearly and in full voice” captures the rhetorical sense.

* “noget trygt og fast at klynge sig til”

  * Something safe and firm to cling to.
  * The phrase anticipates the hymn’s “firm-founded rock.”
  * “something secure and steadfast to cling to” preserves both emotional need and theological stability.

* Hymn:

  * “Hvor finder mit hjerte sin saligste ro? / Hos Jesus, bare hos Jesus!”
  * The hymn answers existential unrest with exclusive reliance on Christ.
  * “blot/bare” means “only,” not merely “simply.”
  * The repeated answer must retain its confessional exclusivity.

STEP 3: ARGUMENT STRUCTURE IDENTIFICATION

* Thesis:

  * The question concerning God cannot finally be expelled from the human soul because the human soul is God’s creature and continues to seek him.

* First narrowing of the question:

  * Humanity does not merely want abstract information about God.
  * The decisive question is whether God cares for us and has a loving heart toward us.

* Existential claim:

  * Until this is certain, the human being cannot have peace.
  * Earthly possessions, gifts, and happiness cannot finally satisfy the soul.
  * The soul longs for God, or more precisely for assurance of being loved by God.

* Personalization:

  * The general question becomes the first-person question of the sinner:
    “Am I, little, wretched, evil, and sinful as I am, loved by God?”
  * This is identified as the fundamental question at the depth of the human heart.

* Insufficiency of human testimony:

  * Other believers may testify from blessed experience that they know themselves loved, graced, and saved.
  * Such testimony is comforting but cannot provide ultimate certainty.
  * Human beings can be mistaken about God and themselves.
  * Therefore subjective experience cannot bear the weight of life and death.

* Christological pivot:

  * There is one who can give a completely trustworthy answer: Jesus Christ.
  * He can answer because he knows God uniquely as the only-begotten Son.
  * John 1:18 supplies the basis of his authority.

* Objective answer:

  * Christ answers the question through the revelation of God’s love:

    * God gave his Son.
    * Heaven rejoices over one sinner who repents.
  * Therefore God does care for sinners.

* Climactic doctrinal assertion:

  * God is love.
  * The Son testifies that God loves us with a love demonstrated in the giving of the Son.
  * This produces the blessed assurance: “God is my Father.”

* Prodigal/Father development:

  * The Father does not forget his child even when the child forgets him, goes his own way, and suffers.
  * This places the theme of Luke 15 at the center of the pastoral argument.

* Missionary transition:

  * This good news is the gospel.
  * Mission exists to make this gospel known as widely as possible.
  * Home Mission specifically seeks to bring it to Norwegian communities in cities and newer settlements.

* Appeal to the reader:

  * The reader is asked to take part in making the gospel known.
  * If the reader has personally known heaven’s joy over a repentant sinner and received assurance of full forgiveness, then that experience becomes a ground of obligation toward others.

* Spiritual-warfare claim:

  * Many brothers and sisters bear wounds caused by the enemy.
  * Only the gospel heals the soul’s burning wounds.
  * Therefore participation in spreading the gospel becomes an act of spiritual mercy.

* Red Cross analogy:

  * Contemporary efforts to relieve bodily suffering through the Red Cross are explicitly praised as blessed work.
  * Yet spiritual distress is declared more grievous than bodily distress.
  * The reader’s own experience of conviction of sin is called as evidence.

* Experiential proof:

  * Under conviction, salvation, grace, and mercy become more important than everything else.
  * Upon seeing the Father’s compassionate heart and believing sins forgiven, the believer becomes joyful amid sorrow, rich amid poverty, happy amid severe adversity.

* Gospel-centered conclusion:

  * This happiness comes through the gospel of salvation from sin and death through Jesus Christ.
  * Therefore the missionary appeal is repeated with increased urgency.

* Historical urgency:

  * The troubled time has produced deep longing for something secure and steadfast.
  * The gospel of God’s love for sinners must therefore sound clearly and fully.

* Hymnic confirmation:

  * The quoted hymn identifies Jesus alone as the heart’s blessed rest and faith’s firm rock.

* Final appeal:

  * The argument terminates not in abstract doctrine but in direct ethical pressure:
    “My brother and sister, do what you can that our people may be led to Jesus! Will you?”

STEP 4: BIBLICAL ECHO IDENTIFICATION

* Luke 15:7

  * Explicitly cited in the heading.
  * “There will be joy in heaven over one sinner who repents.”
  * The larger context is the parables of the lost sheep, lost coin, and lost son.

* Luke 15:11–32

  * Strongly governs the entire Father-child imagery.
  * Especially the child who forgets the Father, goes his own way, suffers, returns repentant, and says, “Father, I have sinned.”

* Luke 15:18, 21

  * Explicit echo in “Father, I have sinned!”

* John 1:18

  * Explicit quotation:
    “The only-begotten Son who is in the Father’s bosom, he has made him known.”
  * Used to establish Christ’s unique authority to reveal God.

* John 3:16

  * Explicit quotation/paraphrase:
    “God so loved the world that he gave his Son.”
  * The giving of the Son functions as objective evidence of divine love.

* 1 John 4:8, 16

  * Echo in “God is love.”
  * Although placed on Jesus’ lips rhetorically by the author, the formula itself belongs directly to 1 John.

* Isaiah 53:6

  * Possible echo in the language of going “one’s own way.”
  * The association is strengthened by the theme of sinful wandering, though Luke 15 is the more immediate framework.

* Ephesians 6:16

  * Likely source for the “arrow” coming from “the enemy.”
  * Paul speaks of the flaming arrows of the evil one.

* Psalmic language of a rock

  * The hymn’s “firm-founded rock for faith” participates broadly in biblical rock imagery associated with God and salvation.
  * Possible resonances include Psalms 18:2; 31:3; 62:2, 6–7; and other passages.

* Isaiah 26:3–4 and related biblical stability imagery

  * Possible broader resonance behind the longing for something “secure and steadfast” and the rock imagery, though the text does not require a single specific source.

* Acts 16:30 and similar salvation-question language

  * “the question of salvation” belongs to a broader biblical vocabulary of the decisive concern, “What must I do to be saved?”
  * This is thematic rather than a direct quotation.

* Philippians 2:12 and recurrent biblical “fear and trembling” language

  * “angst og bæven” resonates with the established biblical formula, though the immediate wording is not necessarily intended as a direct quotation.

* Psalm 51 and prophetic “blotting out” language

  * “all sin blotted out” participates in biblical absolution imagery such as Psalm 51:1, 9 and Isaiah 43:25.

STEP 5: VOICE INTENSITY PRESERVATION

* The opening will remain contemplative but not detached.
* The repeated questions will retain their force as questions addressed to a conscience that cannot escape the issue.
* The self-description “little and wretched ... evil and sinful” will not be softened into general imperfection.
* The inadequacy of subjective religious experience will remain sharp: it comforts, but it cannot furnish final certainty.
* Christ’s answer will be presented as decisive because his knowledge of God is unique.
* “Loves—loves” will be preserved as deliberate intensification.
* The joy of heaven over repentance will retain its direct connection with the individual sinner.
* The spiritual wound imagery will remain severe.
* The comparison between bodily need and spiritual need will preserve the author’s explicit hierarchy.
* The missionary appeal will remain morally implicating: the reader who has received the gospel is summoned to help send it to others.
* The concluding question “Will you?” will remain unembellished, because its simplicity gives it force.

STEP 6: MODAL FORCE PRESERVATION

* “kan ikke godt rives ut” → “cannot readily be torn out,” preserving practical impossibility without overstating absolute metaphysical impossibility.
* “maa jeg faa svar” → “I must have an answer,” preserving necessity.
* “kan gi svar” → “can give an answer,” preserving genuine ability grounded in Christ’s knowledge of God.
* “tør stole paa det” → “may dare to trust it,” preserving the element of ventured reliance rather than reducing it to mere intellectual confidence.
* “skal der være glæde” → “there shall be joy” or “there will be joy.” Because the profile resists inherited KJV gravity, “there will be joy” is preferable while preserving certainty.
* “vil du gjøre dit til” → “will you do your part,” preserving direct ethical appeal.
* “kan læge” → “can heal,” preserving the gospel’s asserted unique efficacy.
* “vær med at sende” → “take part in sending,” retaining imperative force.
* “Det trænges mere end nogensinde før” → “It is needed more than ever before,” preserving urgent necessity.
* “maa ... faa lyde” → “must be allowed to sound forth” or more idiomatically “must sound forth,” preserving necessity.
* “gjør hvad du kan” → “do what you can,” preserving command rather than recommendation.

STEP 10: ORAL CADENCE AWARENESS

* Long sentences will be retained where the argument accumulates.
* Semicolons and dashes will be used to preserve pivots and breath.
* Repeated openings such as “And what...,” “His answer is this...,” and “And you know...” will remain.
* Questions will generally remain in their original sequence rather than being consolidated.
* The two biblical answers will retain parallel presentation.
* The triple contrast “glad amid sorrow, rich amid poverty, happy in the hardest adversity” will retain its oral balance.
* The hymn will remain in four lines so that its responsorial cadence is audible.
* The final two sentences will remain brief after the preceding longer periods, creating the intended concluding pressure.

STEP 11: ACADEMIC TRANSLATION

Joy in Heaven

(Luke 15:7)

The question concerning God cannot readily be torn out of the human soul. Perhaps this comes entirely from the fact that it has come forth from God, created by him. Certainly it may be pressed back by other questions and interests, but it comes again—often strongly and irresistibly.

And what is it that we desire to know? What is there in the question concerning God that is so important for us helpless children of men to have made clear?

Yes, it is this: whether he cares for us, whether he has a loving heart toward us. And until we gain certainty upon this question, we shall never have peace. You who happen to read these words know that this is so. Nothing among all the earthly goods and gifts and earthly happiness you have attained can wholly satisfy you. Your soul longs for something more. And what is it? The answer is: God. Or perhaps more exactly: the certainty of being loved by God.

Am I, little and wretched as I am, evil and sinful as I am—am I loved by God? This is the fundamental question of life at the depth of the human heart, and upon this question I must have an answer. Who can give the answer? Other human beings? O yes, there are people who out of blessed experience can tell us that they know themselves to be loved by God, to have received grace, to be saved. It does us good to meet such people and to listen to their confession. But—it is not enough for me. For these people can be mistaken. Mistaken about God and about themselves. And what they tell me may rest upon a self-assumed delusion or something of that kind.

Is there, then, anyone who can give me an answer so certain and secure that I may dare to trust it, live upon it—die upon it?

Yes, there is. He who speaks in the Bible verse from which the heading of this little piece is taken—he can give an answer—an answer upon which I can rely in life and in death.

And he can give an answer because he knows God as no one else does. “The only-begotten Son, who is in the Father’s bosom, he has made him known.”

He gives his answer to the burning question of the human heart, whether God loves us, out of his own intimate fellowship and knowledge of his Father.

And his answer is this: “God so loved the world that he gave his Son!”

His answer is this: “I say to you: In the same way there will be joy in heaven over one sinner who repents!”

God is love, Jesus says. Thus we have received an answer to the question whether God cares for us. Jesus, his only-begotten Son, says: He loves us—loves, loves so that he gave his Son—gave him to the world, gave him to me!

Glorious truth! Blessed assurance: God is my Father.

I belong with him. And this Father does not forget his child—not even when the child forgets him—goes his own way and comes into misery.

This is the gospel. And the gospel is what the mission seeks to have proclaimed to as many as possible. The gospel is also what our Home Mission desires to have proclaimed to our people throughout the great cities and in the many new settlements where our people are building their homes.

You who happen to read this, will you do your part so that the gospel may be proclaimed to our people? Perhaps you yourself are so blessed as to know that for your sake there has been “joy in heaven”—when, repentant and bowed down, you stood before your God with this prayer: “Father, I have sinned!” and through the gospel received the assurance of full forgiveness of all sin. And is not this assurance the most glorious, the best of all?

O, so many of your brothers and sisters are going about in the world with a soul wounded by the arrow that came from the enemy. The gospel alone can heal the soul’s burning wounds—will you take part in sending it to our people?

At this time, throughout our whole country, collections are being made for “The Red Cross.” What is the purpose? To relieve bodily distress, to lessen pain and misery. Certainly a blessed work! But there is something in the world worse than bodily distress. You know this, my brother and sister, who in deep anguish of soul, in fear and trembling, have stood bowed before God in shame and sorrow over your sin. Everything else in the world seemed so insignificant to you then in comparison with the question of salvation, grace, mercy. And you know this also, that all the other joys and goods you possessed became small in comparison with what you felt, and still feel, when you were permitted to look into the Father’s heart, God’s great, warm, compassionate Father’s heart, and were enabled to believe that all is forgiven, all sin blotted out, remitted. Then you became glad in the midst of sorrow, rich in the midst of poverty, happy in the hardest adversity. And this happiness became yours through the gospel, the message of salvation from sin and death through Jesus Christ. Therefore I will repeat it: take part in sending the gospel out to our people in this country and in Canada! It is needed more than ever before that the message of God’s love for sinners sound forth clearly and in full voice. The time is troubled and difficult, and in many there is stirring a deep longing for something secure and steadfast to cling to:

“Where does my heart find its most blessed rest?
With Jesus, only with Jesus!
Where is found the firm-founded rock for faith?
With Jesus, yes, only with Jesus!”

My brother and sister, do what you can so that our people may be led to Jesus! Will you?

E. B.
