STEP 1: HAZARD IDENTIFICATION

Lexical Hazards

“Møter” refers to organized Christian gatherings for preaching, prayer, edification, and revival, not merely administrative “meetings.” “Meetings” remains the most historically appropriate English rendering.
“Utbytte” denotes the spiritual profit, benefit, or lasting result derived from a meeting. It must not be reduced to enjoyment or immediate emotional response.
“Tro og tanke” joins faith and reflective understanding. The phrase suggests that the meetings should nourish both believing life and Christian thought.
“Erkjendelse av synd og skyld” means a deepened recognition or acknowledgment of both sin and personal guilt before God. “Awareness” alone would be too weak.
“Tilegnelse av naaden, frelsen” refers to the personal appropriation of grace and salvation. It does not merely mean learning about them.
“Barnlig” means childlike in trust and dependence, not childish or immature.
“Delagtiggjøres” means to be made partakers of something. It carries a participatory theological force stronger than simply “receive” or “experience.”
“Guds aand” is God’s Spirit. Where the reference is clearly to the Holy Spirit, “Spirit” must be capitalized.
“Forsagthed” denotes faintheartedness, timidity, or loss of courage in the face of difficulty.
“Godtkjøpstrøst” is literally “cheap consolation”: an easy reassurance used to evade responsibility or failure.
“Dygtiggjort” means made fit, equipped, or rendered capable for a divinely assigned work.
“Aand over et møte” denotes the spiritual character or prevailing spiritual power of a meeting. Rendering it merely as “atmosphere” would secularize the expression.
“Blivende virkninger” means abiding or lasting effects, in contrast to temporary emotional excitement.
“Opbygges” is the established theological term “be edified”: strengthened and built up in Christian faith and life.
“Aandelig bevægelse” means spiritual stirring or spiritual emotion. It includes observable inward agitation, conviction, or feeling, but does not by itself prove conversion.
“Enstydende med” means synonymous with or equivalent to.
“Frelse” must consistently retain its full doctrinal meaning: salvation, not merely improvement, emotional relief, or moral reformation.
“Delagtiggjørelsen i frelsen” means being made a partaker of salvation. The author distinguishes salvation itself from a person’s actual participation in it.
“Viljens hvilelse i harmoni med hans vilje” describes the human will coming to rest in agreement with God’s will. It is more than resignation; it is inward surrender and conformity.
“Naturer” refers to kinds of human temperament or constitution. It should not be modernized into a psychological diagnostic category.
The damaged OCR phrase “b[?] frelst” is almost certainly “bli frelst,” meaning “being saved” or “to be saved.” The translation adopts this reconstruction.

Syntactic Hazards

The prose proceeds through long cumulative sentences in which each clause narrows or develops the preceding claim. Excessive sentence division would weaken the argument.
Semicolons frequently mark theological or rhetorical pivots rather than loose coordination.
The author repeatedly moves from question to anticipated objection, then to concession and correction. These dialogical transitions must remain visible.
Parallel expressions such as “vil noget og vet hvad de vil” depend upon repetition. Replacing the repetition with smoother English would erase the author’s emphasis on definite intention.
Several sentences accumulate purpose clauses introduced by “at.” These should retain their ordered progression rather than being rearranged into topical summaries.
The author frequently places the decisive qualification at the end of a sentence or paragraph. The translation must not anticipate it prematurely.
The transition from page 218 to page 219 interrupts a sentence. The sentence must be restored continuously in English.

Theological Hazards

The author distinguishes spiritual emotion from conversion. Any translation that treats visible emotion as evidence of salvation would reverse his argument.
Salvation is defined positively as surrender of the heart and conformity of the will to God, not merely as fear, regret, or religious feeling.
Prayer is treated not as the performance of a duty but as intentional petition directed toward a definite spiritual end.
The meetings have both evangelical and edifying purposes: the salvation of the unconverted and the building up of God’s children.
The attainable character of the meeting’s high spiritual goal rests upon God’s love for his people and his willingness to give the Spirit.
“The word and the Spirit” must remain distinct but inseparable agencies in the individual’s approach to God.
“Sanctified and made fit” must not be reduced to becoming inspired, encouraged, or more effective in a merely practical sense.
“Sin and guilt before God” must retain its forensic and personal seriousness.
“Grace” and “salvation” are realities to be appropriated in childlike trust, not merely doctrinal subjects to be understood.

Voice Hazards

The author’s tone combines pastoral humility with uncompromising criticism. Either element may be lost if the prose is made merely severe or merely gentle.
“Quietly but firmly: no!” must retain both restraint and decisiveness.
The repeated questions address the conscience of readers who believe that their meetings and prayers are already spiritually adequate.
The author’s concessions—“Yes, of course it is”—do not soften his judgment; they prepare the sharper distinction that follows.
Expressions such as “not deeply enough, not biblically enough” must remain direct and accusatory.
The phrase “cheap consolation” carries deliberate moral pressure and must not be replaced by a neutral term such as “reassurance.”
The prose must remain sober. Its intensity comes through logical consequence rather than emotional embellishment.

STEP 2: IDIOM ANALYSIS

“Den tanke fæstet sig hos mig” literally describes a thought fastening itself upon the writer. It means that the thought has repeatedly taken hold of him and demanded attention.
“Vil noget med sin bøn” means to have a definite intention in one’s prayer. A literal “will something with one’s prayer” would be obscure in English, but the volitional emphasis must remain.
“Slaa av paa kravet” means to reduce, lower, or compromise the demand because the goal appears difficult.
“Det er ikke saa nøie” means that it supposedly does not matter very much. Here it functions as an evasive spiritual excuse.
“Som barn omkring sin fars bord” invokes the household relation of children gathered around their father’s table. It suggests belonging, dependence, nourishment, fellowship, and filial confidence before God.
“Herren har sit folk kjært” means that the Lord loves or holds his people dear. It has biblical resonance and supports the argument that God is willing to bless his gathered people.
“Aand over et møte” is a traditional revivalist and pietistic expression for the perceptible spiritual power or character of a gathering. “Atmosphere” would obscure its theological content.
“Frelse om ikke mere end én sjæl” is a familiar revivalist prayer formula: that the Lord would save at least one soul, even if no more than one. The author questions whether those who use the formula understand what salvation entails.
“Lægge for dagen” means to manifest, disclose, or show openly.
“Grepne av ordets virkning” means seized or inwardly affected by the working of the word. It suggests that the preached word has laid hold upon the hearer.
“Saalangt det rækker” means “so far as it reaches” or “within its proper limits.” The author grants emotional stirring a real but limited value.
“Båret sig dumt ad” literally means to have behaved foolishly. Here it signifies distress over one’s bad conduct without necessarily involving repentance before God.
“Jeg vil staa op og gaa til min Fader” is a direct echo of Luke 15:18. The traditional English biblical form is “I will arise and go to my father.” Because the translation is to avoid inherited King James diction, “I will rise and go to my Father” preserves the structure and gravity without using an archaic register.
“Baner ordet sig vei gjennem følelsen til viljen” presents the word as making or clearing a path through the emotions until it reaches and influences the will. The metaphor must remain intact.
“Opbygges” corresponds naturally to the biblical and ecclesial term “edified.” Modern paraphrases such as “feel encouraged” would be too narrow.
“Guds barn” is the established pietistic expression “God’s children,” denoting believers in their filial relation to God.

STEP 3: ARGUMENT STRUCTURE IDENTIFICATION

The author first sets aside the question of declining attendance so that he may examine the intrinsic spiritual quality of the meetings.
He establishes his governing premise: Christian meetings are held with an intended purpose and must therefore be judged according to whether that purpose is attained.
He defines the intended purposes of the meetings:
profit for faith and thought
a nearer approach to God through the word and the Spirit
deeper recognition of sin and guilt
more childlike appropriation of grace and salvation
growth in Christian knowledge
deeper insight into the Lord’s word and plan of salvation
clearer understanding of Christian life and vocation
participation in a greater fullness of God’s Spirit
He acknowledges that this goal is high but denies that it is too high or unattainable.
He rejects two improper responses to difficulty:
ceasing to hold such meetings
lowering the spiritual demand and consoling oneself that the outcome does not matter
He grounds confidence in God’s disposition:
the Lord loves his people
the Lord willingly gives the Spirit
the Lord is willing to bless his children when they gather to be sanctified and equipped
He then introduces the experiential problem: the actual spiritual profit of many meetings is small.
He defines genuine profit negatively and positively:
it is not merely strong emotion
it includes spiritual power in the meeting
deeper entrance into the word
lasting effects of the preached word
He modestly limits his claim: he cannot identify every hindrance but hopes to awaken attention and remove at least some causes.
He identifies the first major deficiency: insufficient preparation through prayer.
He anticipates the objection that believers already pray for meetings.
He rejects the mere existence of prayer as proof of adequate preparation.
He argues that many prayers lack definite intention:
some pray only from duty
others desire something but do not know precisely what they desire
He examines the common request that sinners be saved and believers edified.
He confesses that he himself has prayed in this conventional manner without clearly understanding his petition.
He identifies the likely hidden meaning of such prayer: a desire for publicly visible spiritual emotion, especially among previously indifferent people.
He grants that such emotion is good and can bring great joy.
He then makes the central distinction: spiritual stirring is not identical with salvation.
He explains that visible emotion may be genuine or superficial and deceptive.
He rebukes shallow and insufficiently biblical conceptions of salvation.
He defines participation in salvation as:
the heart’s surrender to God
the will’s rest in harmony with God’s will
the sinner’s inward resolution to return to the Father
He finally restores spiritual emotion to its proper place:
it should be desired and prayed for
it is especially valuable when produced by living, Spirit-filled preaching
the word commonly reaches the will through feeling
nevertheless, spiritual emotion must not be overvalued

STEP 4: BIBLICAL ECHO IDENTIFICATION

“Through the word and the Spirit” reflects the broad biblical conjunction of divine revelation and the Spirit’s inward work. Possible backgrounds include John 6:63, John 16:13, and passages describing the Spirit’s work through proclaimed truth.
“Deeper recognition of sin and guilt before God” echoes the Law’s disclosure of sin in Romans 3:20 and the Spirit’s conviction of sin in John 16:8.
“Childlike and trusting appropriation of grace” evokes the biblical language of receiving the kingdom as a child, especially Mark 10:15 and Luke 18:17.
“The Lord has his people dear” may echo Deuteronomy 33:3, where the Lord’s love for his people is affirmed, though the wording may also reflect general biblical and devotional language rather than a single exact quotation.
“No gift is the Lord more willing to give his people than the gift of the Spirit” strongly recalls Luke 11:13: the heavenly Father gives the Holy Spirit to those who ask him.
The image of God’s people gathered “as children around their Father’s table” combines biblical household imagery with the Father’s readiness to give good gifts in Matthew 7:11 and Luke 11:11–13. It may also carry an ecclesial or sacramental resonance, though that is not made explicit.
“Sanctified and made fit for their work” recalls 2 Timothy 2:21, where a purified vessel is sanctified and made useful to the master, prepared for every good work.
“God’s children may be edified” echoes New Testament language concerning the building up of the church, especially Acts 20:32, 1 Corinthians 14, and Ephesians 4:11–16.
The word’s observable effect upon hearers recalls Acts 2:37, where hearers are cut to the heart, but the author warns that visible agitation alone does not prove conversion.
“I will rise and go to my Father” directly echoes the prodigal son’s resolution in Luke 15:18.
The heart’s surrender and the will’s harmony with God’s will are consonant with Romans 12:1–2 and the petition “your will be done,” though the author does not directly quote those passages.
The distinction between transient response and enduring fruit reflects the biblical concern that the word take lasting root, especially in the parable of the sower in Matthew 13, Mark 4, and Luke 8.

STEP 5: VOICE INTENSITY PRESERVATION

The opening must remain searching rather than merely descriptive: the author is questioning whether the meetings are what they ought to be.
The statement that meetings may be held without any real purpose must retain its implicit rebuke.
The high goal must be presented as binding, not aspirational in a modern motivational sense.
“Cheap consolation” must remain morally discrediting.
The assurance that the goal can be attained must not become vague optimism; it is grounded in God’s love and willingness to give the Spirit.
The meetings must remain described as “ineffectual” and “poor in spirit and profit.” Softer language would diminish the diagnosis.
“Quietly but firmly: no!” must preserve restrained authority.
The critique of prayer must retain its full force: many pray from duty, without definite desire or clear understanding.
The author’s self-implication must remain. He does not accuse others from a distance but confesses that he has prayed in the same unreflective manner.
“Not deeply enough, not biblically enough” must remain unsoftened.
Hell must remain explicitly named. The author distinguishes true salvation even from terror of hell.
The final caution must preserve both joy and restraint: spiritual stirring is welcomed, but it is not to be treated as decisive proof.

STEP 6: MODAL FORCE PRESERVATION

“Jeg skal gaa det forbi” expresses a deliberate decision: “I shall pass over that.”
“Hvad de burde være” carries moral obligation: “what they ought to be.”
“Maa paavirkes” occurs in a purpose construction and is best rendered “may be influenced,” not “must be influenced.”
“Bør hverken skræmme os” retains obligation: the difficulty “ought neither to frighten us.”
“Maa naaes” in the prayer for the goal expresses desired attainment: “that it may be attained.”
“Det kan naaes” expresses real possibility: “it can be attained.”
“Du maa frelse syndere” has strong petitionary force. “You must save sinners at this meeting” preserves the urgency and exposes the author’s question about what the petitioners actually mean.
“Guds barn maa opbygges” occurs under “we pray that” and therefore expresses the desired result: “that God’s children may be edified.”
“En sjæl maa bli frelst” likewise belongs to a prayer formula and is rendered “that a soul may be saved.”
“Vi skal attrå og bede” retains obligation: “we should desire and pray.”
“Kan vi vanskelig faa formeget av” expresses a strong positive judgment: “we can hardly have too much of it.”
The contrast “kan være … men kan også være” must retain equal possibility: the emotion may be deep and true, but may also be superficial and deceptive.

STEP 11: ACADEMIC TRANSLATION

A Few Words about the Lutheran Free Church and Its Tasks

E. B.

—

III.

There could be much to say about the reason why our meetings are less well attended than before; but I shall pass over that this time and look somewhat at our meetings as we generally have them.

At times the thought has taken hold of me that our meetings are not always what they ought to be. And on this account the question has arisen: what is the deficiency, or what are the deficiencies, in several of our meetings?

As will be understood, I proceed from the assumption that we intend something, that we will something, when we hold meetings.

For it is also conceivable that meetings may be held without anything real being intended by them.

But what is it, then, that we will?

The answer may perhaps be given: we wish, if possible, to derive some profit for faith and thought. We intend that the individual, through the word and the Spirit, may come nearer to God. Among other things, we intend through our meetings that those who gather may be influenced by the truth toward a deeper recognition of sin and guilt before God, and toward a more childlike and trusting appropriation of grace, of salvation.

And finally, we intend to impart Christian knowledge, deeper insight into the Lord’s word and the Lord’s plan of salvation for the individual and for the congregation, and a clearer understanding of our life and of the task we have been set to accomplish here in life. In a word: we would so gladly be made partakers of a greater fullness of God’s Spirit.

As may be seen, the goal here set as the result of a meeting is rather high. But not too high. It is not unattainable, even though it is not easy to reach. Yet the difficulty involved in attaining this high goal ought neither to frighten us away from holding such meetings nor, in faintheartedness, cause us to lower the demand or comfort ourselves with the cheap consolation that it matters little whether the goal is reached or not.

No, let the high goal stand before our thought and our longing in all its beauty and greatness; and let us, with all the means God himself has entrusted to us, labor and pray that it may be attained.

The truth is, namely, that it can be attained. For of all that is certain, nothing is more certain than this, that “the Lord holds his people dear.” No gift is the Lord more willing to give his people than the gift of the Spirit. And when his people gather as children around their Father’s table to be sanctified and made fit for their work for him, the Lord is willing to bless.

It is, however, a fact that the experience of many concerning the profit of such meetings is this: that it has often been so small. And by profit I do not mean primarily the more or less powerful stirring which, for many, is the only profit they desire. No, I think rather of what is called spirit in a meeting, of whether we succeed in leading one another deeper into the word, and finally of the abiding effects of the word spoken during the meeting.

What, then, is the reason that the profit is so often so small? What is the reason that many of our meetings are so ineffectual, so poor in spirit and in profit for the participants?

The causes are no doubt many, perhaps. And I do not presume to think that I possess the ability to point out all the hindering causes on account of which several of our meetings bear so little fruit. But if I could succeed in drawing the attention of our Free Church people to this matter, and if I could moreover point out some of these hindering causes and, if possible, do ever so little toward their removal, the purpose of these words would be attained.

There are certain things in connection with our larger meetings that I wish to mention, but I am uncertain which I should mention first. I therefore point to the hindrances I believe I see in the order in which they present themselves to my thought.

The first great deficiency in at least most of our meetings, I believe, is this: they are far too little prepared through prayer.

I know well that some who read this will object that this is not so, but that believers, prior to the holding of a meeting, pray for God’s blessing upon it. Yes, at almost all the meetings I have attended, I myself have heard speakers say that they have asked the Lord to “save, if only one soul, at the meeting.” And this, then, is supposed to be proof that the meeting has been well prepared through prayer? To such a claim I will say, quietly but firmly: no!

For in the first place, very few of those who pray for a meeting intend anything definite by their prayer. They pray because they believe it is their duty to do so. Or, if they do intend something by their prayer, there are many who do not know what they intend. And how, then, can God answer such a prayer?

There are no doubt those who will contradict me here also and answer that they both intend something and know what they intend. For, they say, we ask the Lord to save the unsaved, and we pray that God’s children may be edified; and then surely we must intend something and know what it is we intend.

Well, yes, I myself have many times prayed in the same manner, but I fear that, nevertheless, I have not truly been clear about what I was asking. What is meant when in prayer we say to God: you must save sinners at this meeting?

I assume that the very great majority who pray in this manner think chiefly of a spiritual stirring perceptible to everyone during the meeting, especially among those who were assumed until then to have been indifferent.

Yes, someone says, but is this not good?

Yes, of course it is. And more than once during the time in which I have taken part in meetings, I myself have experienced the great joy of witnessing such a stirring. And yet—it is not decisive.

Spiritual stirring is not synonymous with “being saved.” What is decisive—the fact that a soul in its inmost being turns from the world and sin to God—is not proved by an hour of emotional stirring during a meeting. The stirring that is perceived may be deep and true, but it may also be superficial and deceptive.

It is therefore not certain that all who pray that a “soul may be saved” during the meeting are thinking of, and truly know, what they are asking. They may pray earnestly and mean well, but they do not think deeply enough, not biblically enough. For as a rule, by the expression “the salvation of a soul,” one thinks only of being allowed, at the meeting, to witness one or more persons, with tears and perhaps with a request for intercession, show openly that they have been seized by the effect of the word. And this—as already said—is good and well so far as it reaches. But with many, with most, it does not reach far enough.

Salvation, or perhaps more properly, being made a partaker of salvation, is something else and something more—not merely religious moods, not merely anguish over having acted foolishly, and still less terror of hell, but the heart’s surrender to God, the will at rest in harmony with his will, so that the sinner in the inmost depth of his being says: “I will rise and go to my Father.”

By this I have not said that we should not desire and pray for spiritual stirring during a meeting. No, we can hardly have too much of it—at least not of that stirring which is brought about by a living and Spirit-filled proclamation of God’s word.

For it is a truth confirmed by experience that the word most often makes its way through feeling to the will.

There may, of course, be such natures whose life of the will is best influenced through thought, but they are exceptions, not the rule. Therefore let us rejoice that spiritual stirring is perceptible during a meeting, but let us not overvalue it.
