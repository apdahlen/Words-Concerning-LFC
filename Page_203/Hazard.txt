STEP 1: HAZARD IDENTIFICATION

Lexical Hazards

* “delagtiggjøres” means to be brought into participation in something or enabled to share in it. In this theological context, it concerns receiving divine blessings rather than merely acquiring benefits.

* “goder” literally means goods or benefits, but here carries the sense of spiritual blessings. Rendering it as “goods” would sound material and obscure the religious context.

* “aand” may refer to the human spirit, spiritual vitality, or the Holy Spirit. The referent must be determined in each occurrence. Lowercase “aand” in “fattige paa aand” denotes spiritual life or vitality, whereas “hans aand” in the quotation from Ephesians denotes the Holy Spirit.

* “vor aands fattigdom” is spiritual poverty in a negative sense. It must not be confused with the positive biblical expression “poor in spirit” in Matthew 5:3.

* “lunkenhet” is lukewarmness, with a direct theological association to the church of Laodicea. It denotes culpable spiritual complacency, not merely diminished enthusiasm.

* “gudsforhold” means one’s relation to God or life in relation to God. “Standing with God” would risk introducing a narrower forensic meaning not required by the passage.

* “nødrop” is a cry arising from acute need or distress. It is stronger than an ordinary request.

* “samdrægtig” means unitedly or with one accord. It emphasizes spiritual agreement and common purpose, not merely physical proximity.

* “lønkammeret” is the private chamber used as an image of secret prayer, echoing Matthew 6:6. A literal modern rendering such as “bedroom” would be misleading.

* “enkeltmandskristendom” means a Christianity conceived as belonging only to isolated individuals.

* “samfundskristendom” means Christianity lived in fellowship or communion. It does not mean institutional religion or political collectivism.

* “samfund” normally means fellowship or communion in this passage. In the Apostles’ Creed, “de helliges samfund” is properly rendered “the communion of saints.”

* “gudsliv” is the believer’s life in God. It must retain its explicitly theological content.

* “naadegaver” means gifts of grace. “Talents” or merely “abilities” would weaken the divine source of the gifts.

* “ensomhet søkes” means that solitude is to be deliberately sought as a spiritual discipline.

* “fri samvirken” denotes voluntary cooperation among free and self-governing congregations. “Free” refers to ecclesiastical liberty, not to lack of cost.

* “frie møter” likewise refers to meetings arising from the free spiritual cooperation of the congregations. The phrase should not be modernized into “informal events.”

* “menighetsengelen” is the angel of the congregation or church addressed in Revelation. It may carry the traditional interpretive sense of the congregation’s spiritual representative, but the biblical image must remain intact.

* “kjøpe av ham” echoes Revelation 3:18. It describes receiving from Christ what spiritual poverty requires; it must not be explained away as a merely commercial metaphor.

Syntactic Hazards

* The prose repeatedly uses long sentences in which the argument accumulates through subordinate clauses. Breaking these into short modern sentences would weaken the sermonic reasoning.

* Several sentences employ a resumed subject: “He who alone can give us … he has said.” This is part of the oral cadence and should be preserved where possible.

* The author places short, emphatic sentences after longer reflections: “Yes, that is dangerous.” Such abrupt sentences carry moral force and must not be absorbed into surrounding paragraphs.

* Semicolons often mark genuine argumentative pivots rather than optional pauses.

* Rhetorical questions are not requests for neutral information. They place the reader’s conscience under examination.

* Biblical quotations are woven directly into the argument. They should not be detached into separate proof texts or reformatted as scholarly citations.

* Parallel constructions such as “always more, always farther onward” and “both individually and as congregations” must retain their balance.

* The repeated movement from “the individual” to “the congregation” is structurally important and must not be generalized into a single undifferentiated Christian subject.

* The final Laodicean question remains syntactically suspended around an incomplete biblical quotation. Its unsettled form intensifies the rebuke and should not be made overly neat.

Theological Hazards

* The statement that the gifts are not received “without our doing something” must not be expanded into a doctrine of meritorious works. The subsequent context defines the human response as acknowledgment of need, humiliation, prayer, fellowship, and seeking.

* Spiritual poverty and lukewarmness are treated as conditions that must be confessed, not as morally neutral emotional states.

* Paul’s dissatisfaction concerns continued spiritual growth and striving, not uncertainty regarding justification or God’s faithfulness.

* “Power” is repeatedly divine power given through the Holy Spirit, not confidence, organizational efficiency, or human energy.

* Prayer is presented as a divinely established means through which spiritual blessing reaches both individuals and congregations.

* The text distinguishes personal reception of salvation from the communal form in which Christian life is intended to be lived. Neither individual faith nor ecclesial fellowship may be erased.

* “Union with God” must retain its theological seriousness and must not be weakened to “feeling close to God.”

* The communion of saints is treated as an article of faith and as the necessary form of the congregation’s life.

* Christian unity is connected directly to the credibility and spread of the gospel through the prayer of Christ in John 17.

* “Gifts of grace” are necessary for the building of the Free Church; they are not presented as optional enhancements.

* The warning to Laodicea exposes false spiritual self-sufficiency. The contrast between professed wealth and actual wretchedness must remain severe.

Voice Hazards

* The author’s repeated “perhaps” does not signal indifference. It is a restrained rhetorical method that draws the reader toward an unavoidable judgment.

* The direct address “Brothers and sisters” must retain its pastoral earnestness.

* The author includes himself in the indictment through “we” and “us.” The translation must not turn the passage into criticism directed only at others.

* Expressions such as “our half-hearted Christianity” and “our great loss” must not be softened into general observations about institutional challenges.

* The Laodicean warning must retain its accusatory force without becoming theatrical.

* The concluding “Oh” is a genuine cry of exhortation, not sentimental embellishment.

* The prose alternates between confession, doctrinal instruction, warning, and appeal. Excessive smoothing would erase these shifts.

STEP 2: IDIOM ANALYSIS

* “trøster sig med” means to console or reassure oneself by means of a thought. Here it describes false reassurance grounded in the assumption that the needed blessings are already possessed.

* “slaa os til ro med” means to settle into complacent acceptance of a condition. A literal translation involving sitting or lying down would obscure the moral implication.

* “en vei at gaa” is both an ordinary idiom and a spiritual image. Acknowledging poverty and lukewarmness is described as a difficult road involving humiliation and suffering.

* “den kvier vi os for” means that we shrink from, recoil from, or are reluctant to undergo something.

* “bestandig mere, bestandig længere frem” expresses unceasing spiritual advance. Its parallel form resembles the accumulative exhortation of revival and devotional preaching.

* “gribe os, fylde os” depicts conviction as something that seizes and fills the whole person. It should remain figurative.

* “kraften fra det høie” is a biblical expression for power from God, especially the empowering gift of the Holy Spirit. It echoes Luke 24:49.

* “klage dem for Herren” means to bring one’s helplessness and need before the Lord in lament. “Complain to the Lord” would suggest petulance rather than prayerful lament.

* “hjertet kan bli koldt, knærne stive” is a paired spiritual image. A cold heart signifies diminished love and spiritual vitality; stiff knees signify the neglect or difficulty of prayer.

* “Herrens store barneflok” portrays believers as the Lord’s great company or family of children. The domestic and relational image should remain.

* “det er ikke til at ta feil av” means there can be no mistaking the matter. It introduces a claim the author regards as unmistakably established by the New Testament.

* “holde fast ved” means to adhere faithfully, persevere in, or remain devoted to something. In Acts 2:42 it describes continuing steadfastly in the apostolic practices.

* “kjærlighetens baand” is the biblical and ecclesial image of the bonds of love. It suggests a unity formed by the Spirit rather than imposed by organization.

* “løse vor opgave” means to fulfill or carry out the task entrusted to the church. “Solve our task” would be unidiomatic in English.

* “trangen til at komme sammen” is more than a casual preference. It is the felt spiritual need or inward impulse to gather.

* “kjøpe av ham” echoes Christ’s counsel in Revelation 3:18. The paradox is that those who are spiritually poor must obtain true riches from Christ himself.

Natural biblical English equivalents

* “samdrægtig” naturally corresponds to “with one accord,” familiar from older English translations of Acts. It remains intelligible without archaic pronouns.

* “kraft fra det høie” naturally corresponds to “power from on high.”

* “de helliges samfund” corresponds to the established creedal expression “the communion of saints.”

* “Jeg jager efter at gribe det” may be rendered “I press on to lay hold of it,” preserving the movement of Philippians 3 without adopting archaic diction.

* “jeg iler mot maalet” may be rendered “I hasten toward the goal.”

* “lønkammeret” has the traditional equivalent “the closet,” but “the private chamber” better avoids an unintended modern meaning while retaining the biblical allusion.

* “De holdt samdrægtig ved i bønnen” may be rendered “They continued with one accord in prayer.”

Expressions requiring more than strict literalism

* “fattige paa aand” should be rendered “poor in spiritual life” rather than simply “poor in spirit,” because the latter may wrongly suggest the beatitude.

* “samfundskristendom” requires “a Christianity of fellowship” or a similar formulation. “Community Christianity” would sound sociological.

* “gudsliv som samfundsliv” is best rendered “their life in God as a life in fellowship,” preserving both elements of the compound expression.

* “frie møter” should remain “free meetings,” but its ecclesiastical meaning arises from the surrounding discussion of free, independent congregations.

STEP 3: ARGUMENT STRUCTURE IDENTIFICATION

* The section begins with the governing question: How may the church come to share in the indispensable spiritual blessings previously discussed?

* A possible complacent answer is raised and rejected: some may assume that the blessings are already possessed and require no concern.

* The author then establishes a common diagnosis: both personal faith and congregational life are marked by profound spiritual poverty.

* The question is restated in practical form: How can the church receive what it needs?

* The first principle is asserted: the gifts are not given against the recipients’ will, nor are they received without a genuine response.

* The first required response is confession—acknowledgment of spiritual poverty and lukewarmness.

* The author immediately identifies the cost of such acknowledgment: humiliation and suffering, from which people naturally shrink.

* A warning follows against complacent acceptance of personal and congregational spiritual conditions.

* Paul is introduced as the counterexample. His life is characterized by continual striving, and he summons the congregations to the same pursuit.

* The argument turns from diagnosis to promise: if the recognition of powerlessness truly seized the church, cries for help would arise, and help would be given.

* The first congregation becomes the model. Christ promises the Holy Spirit’s power, and the congregation perseveres together in prayer until Pentecost.

* Prayer is then applied directly to the Free Church. Christ has commanded asking, and spiritual blessing is said to reach neither individuals nor congregations without earnest prayer.

* The author exposes a concrete deficiency: too little prayer in private and in congregational gatherings.

* The argument then broadens from prayer to ecclesiology. Christianity is not intended to remain an isolated individual possession but to be lived as the communion of saints.

* Personal reception of salvation is affirmed, but individual isolation is denied as the intended form of Christian life.

* The New Testament’s emphasis on fellowship is connected to Christ’s prayer that his people may be one.

* Unity is then given missionary consequence: the world’s belief and the progress of Christianity depend upon it.

* The author distinguishes the appropriate spiritual path for the individual and for the congregation. The individual may need solitude; the congregation must seek fellowship.

* Acts 2:42 supplies the apostolic pattern of teaching, fellowship, breaking of bread, and prayers.

* Sverdrup is cited to apply this principle to free and independent congregations. Their liberty does not remove the necessity of spiritual connection and cooperation.

* Free meetings and gatherings are presented as the practical means by which relationships, shared understanding, love, and common work are sustained.

* The author then introduces a present concern: attendance and desire for annual, mission, and district meetings appear to be declining.

* The final rhetorical question raises the gravest possible explanation—Laodicean self-satisfaction.

* The section ends not merely with accusation but with an appeal: the readers are to accept Christ’s counsel and obtain from him what they lack.

STEP 4: BIBLICAL ECHO IDENTIFICATION

* “I press on to lay hold of it” echoes Philippians 3:12.

* “I hasten toward the goal” echoes Philippians 3:14.

* “You also, brothers, become imitators of me” most likely echoes Philippians 3:17, with possible resonance from 1 Corinthians 4:16 and 11:1.

* “Therefore I bow my knees before the Father … that he … may grant you to be strengthened with power through his Spirit in your inner being” is from Ephesians 3:14–16.

* “You will receive power when the Holy Spirit comes upon you, and you will be my witnesses … to the ends of the earth” is from Acts 1:8.

* “They continued with one accord in prayer” echoes Acts 1:14.

* “Power from on high” echoes Luke 24:49 and is connected here with the Pentecost account in Acts 2.

* “Ask, and you will receive” echoes Matthew 7:7, Luke 11:9, and John 16:24.

* “I know your works” is the recurring formula of the messages to the seven churches in Revelation 2–3.

* The private chamber of prayer echoes Matthew 6:6.

* “The communion of saints” is the language of the Third Article of the Apostles’ Creed, grounded in the New Testament doctrine of the church.

* “That they may be one” and “so that the world may believe that you have sent me” are from John 17:21.

* “They held faithfully to the apostles’ teaching and to the fellowship, to the breaking of bread and to the prayers” is Acts 2:42.

* “The bonds of love” has broad New Testament resonance, especially Colossians 3:14 and Ephesians 4:3.

* “Rich … lack nothing … wretched and pitiable and poor and blind and naked” is from Revelation 3:17.

* “I counsel you to buy from me” is from Revelation 3:18.

STEP 5: VOICE INTENSITY PRESERVATION

* The confession “we are unspeakably poor” must remain comprehensive and personal.

* “Yes, that is dangerous” must remain abrupt. Expanding it into explanation would reduce its force.

* “Our powerlessness and half-hearted Christianity” must remain accusatory and must continue to include the author and reader together.

* “Cries for help” should retain the sense that real recognition of need necessarily produces desperate prayer.

* “Our great loss” must not be softened to an administrative weakness or an area for improvement.

* The assertion that Christianity’s progress and spread depend upon unity must remain categorical.

* The decline in the desire to gather must be treated as spiritually alarming, not merely as a logistical or generational trend.

* The Laodicean quotation must expose the contradiction between professed abundance and actual destitution.

* The final appeal must preserve both urgency and dependence upon Christ: the answer is not self-reform alone, but receiving from him.

STEP 6: MODAL FORCE PRESERVATION

* “maa vistnok erkjende” requires “must surely acknowledge,” not “may recognize.”

* “Vi faar aldrig” is categorical: “We never receive.”

* “kan vi gjøre?” concerns real capacity: “What can we do?”

* “om vi bare vil” concerns willingness: “provided only that we are willing.”

* “tør hænde” is cautious but real possibility: “it may be.”

* “vilde der bli nødrop” is a counterfactual consequence: if recognition seized the church, cries for help would arise.

* “I skal faa kraft” is a firm promise: “You will receive power.”

* “Vi kan, om vi vil” preserves both capacity and moral willingness: “We can, if we are willing.”

* “de maa være ett” expresses the intended and prayed-for unity: “they may be one.” In the biblical quotation, “may” expresses petition rather than uncertainty.

* “hans folk skal leve” expresses divine intention: God’s people are to live their life in fellowship.

* “Frikirken ikke kan bygges” expresses impossibility without the gifts: the Free Church cannot be built without them.

* “Skal frie, selvstændige menigheter ikke tape” expresses necessary condition: “If free, independent congregations are not to lose…”

* “maa vi forstaa” and “maa … kjende” require “must.”

* “vil … næsten være umulig” is a strong considered judgment: “will be almost impossible.”

* “den kraft … som vi maa ha” requires “the power … which we must have.”

* “om vi skal kunne løse vor opgave” expresses necessary empowerment for fulfilling the task: “if we are to be able to fulfill our task.”

STEP 8: METAPHOR INTEGRITY PRESERVATION

* Spiritual lack is represented as poverty. This image continues into the Laodicean warning and must remain coherent.

* Conviction is something that may seize and fill the congregation.

* Prayer arises as a cry for help from those who have recognized their helplessness.

* Divine empowerment descends as power from on high.

* The heart grows cold, and the knees grow stiff. Both physical images must remain visible.

* The church is the Lord’s great company of children.

* Christian life is something lived “in God.”

* The Free Church is something built, and the gifts of grace are indispensable to that building.

* Love forms bonds that are tied in the freedom of the Spirit.

* The Laodiceans claim riches but are exposed as poor, blind, and naked.

* Christ’s command to “buy” from him must retain its paradoxical economic imagery.

STEP 9: TONE CONTRAST PRESERVATION

* The opening tone is searching and deliberative.

* It moves into sober confession of spiritual poverty.

* The warning against complacency becomes sharp and abrupt.

* Paul’s example introduces disciplined forward movement.

* The Pentecost narrative brings promise and hope, but only through persevering prayer.

* The discussion of deficient prayer returns to rebuke.

* The teaching on the communion of saints becomes constructive and doctrinal.

* Christ’s prayer for unity gives the passage an elevated and missionary seriousness.

* Sverdrup’s statement introduces practical ecclesiastical counsel.

* Concern over declining gatherings darkens the tone again.

* The Laodicean quotation reaches the passage’s severest point.

* The final appeal introduces hope without weakening the warning: Christ possesses what the church lacks and counsels it to receive from him.

STEP 10: ORAL CADENCE AWARENESS

* Long sentences will be retained where claims accumulate through cause, contrast, and consequence.

* Short declarations will remain short when they function as spoken blows to the conscience.

* Repeated structures such as “always more, always farther onward” will preserve their parallel rhythm.

* Semicolons will mark turns within a continuous spoken argument.

* Direct questions will retain their interrogative pressure.

* Scriptural words will remain embedded within the sermon’s flow.

* The alternation between “we,” “the individual,” and “the congregation” will remain audible.

* The resumed subject in “He who alone can give us … he has said” will be preserved.

* The repeated phrase “if we are willing” will maintain the connection between spiritual possibility and moral responsibility.

* The final “Oh” will remain as an oral appeal rather than being removed as stylistically unnecessary.

STEP 11: ACADEMIC TRANSLATION

A Few Words on the Lutheran Free Church and Its Tasks

C. B.

[ornament]

II.

The question, then, is this: how can we come to share in these blessings, so glorious and indispensable as they are for us?

There may perhaps be one person or another who, without further thought, consoles himself with the belief that we possess them, and therefore need not concern ourselves with this matter, either for ourselves or for others.

I do not think, however, that very many think in this way. Most among us must surely acknowledge that we are unspeakably poor in spiritual life and in everything else that we so deeply need, both in our personal life of faith and in our congregational life.

We therefore feel the importance of the question: how can we receive what we need? It is perhaps not so easy to answer this question.

I believe, however, that many agree with me when, to begin with, I answer: We never receive these blessings and gifts against our will, nor do we receive them without doing something in order to receive them.

Yes, but what can we do? Perhaps not much; but we can do something, provided only that we are willing. We can, first of all, acknowledge our spiritual poverty and our lukewarmness. But this is by no means an easy road to walk. It costs humiliation and suffering, and from that we shrink.

I do not know for certain, but it may be that there has been some inclination among us to settle into complacency both with our own spiritual condition and with that of our congregations. Yes, that is dangerous.

Paul was never satisfied, either with his own relation to God or with that of the congregations. Always more, always farther onward: “I press on to lay hold of it”—“I hasten toward the goal.”

And he exhorts the congregations to which he writes his letters to do likewise: “You also, brothers, become imitators of me!” “Therefore I bow my knees before the Father … that he … may grant you to be strengthened with power through his Spirit in your inner being.”

Yes, if this deep acknowledgment of our powerlessness and our half-hearted Christianity could seize us, fill us! Then there would be cries for help, and then we would receive help.

To his first congregation Jesus said: “You will receive power when the Holy Spirit comes upon you, and you will be my witnesses both in Jerusalem and to the ends of the earth.”

And of this congregation it is written: “They continued with one accord in prayer.” And they continued in this prayer with one accord until, on the day of Pentecost, they received the power from on high.

He who alone can give us what we most need, also in our Free Church work—he has said to us as well: “Ask, and you will receive!” We can, if we are willing, acknowledge our helplessness and need and lament them before the Lord. We can tell him at least something about what is lacking—not first and foremost in others, but in ourselves, both individually and as congregations.

[Page 204]

Certainly he knows it well, he who says, “I know your works,” without our telling him; but he has nevertheless laid down in his word the rule that spiritual blessing reaches neither the individual nor the congregation without earnest prayer. And here, no doubt, lies our great loss: We pray so little, both in the private chamber and in the congregational gathering. I know well that believing people can never cease praying altogether; but I also know that the heart can grow cold, the knees stiff, and prayer many a time become only a prayer for ourselves.

Brothers and sisters, let us attend to this matter. For it is so important.

The kingdom of God that Jesus brought was not intended to be merely a Christianity of individuals. It was intended to be a Christianity of fellowship, in the sense in which we confess in the Third Article: “I believe in the communion of saints.”

It is certainly true that it is as individuals that we come to share in salvation and enter into union with God; but it is not intended that each believer should live his life in God for himself alone, but rather that he should live this life in the blessed consciousness of belonging among the Lord’s great company of children.

According to the New Testament, there can be no mistaking that it is God’s will and purpose that his people should live their life in God as a life in fellowship. Therefore so much is said about “fellowship.”

And Jesus prays for his own that “they may be one” with almost greater earnestness than for anything else.

We may therefore conclude that, for him, this matter has overwhelmingly great importance. Yes, he himself says that he prays for this “so that the world may believe that you have sent me.”

The progress and spread of Christianity depend upon this.

It is therefore important to think of the congregation and the congregations when the question is how we are to receive the gifts of grace without which the Free Church cannot be built. And when it concerns the manner in which these gifts may be obtained, the way is not simply the same for the individual as for the congregation. For the individual, perhaps one of the most important things required in order to receive these gifts is that solitude be sought. For the congregation, on the other hand, what is required, as already pointed out, is that fellowship be sought—the communion of saints.

Even after the outpouring of the Spirit, it is said of the first Christians: “They held faithfully to the apostles’ teaching and to the fellowship, to the breaking of bread and to the prayers.” Acts 2:42.

They therefore found it necessary to gather with one another in such assemblies, where love for one another and love for the Lord and his cause were the sole command impelling them to gather.

Concerning this matter, the late Professor Sverdrup says in Collected Writings, volume 3, page 307:

“If free, independent congregations are not to lose something of the power of cooperation, spiritual connection is needed. Free cooperation in everything that can serve the advancement of God’s kingdom at home and abroad is necessary. If it is to take place, then we must understand one another and know one another. We must exchange thoughts and spirit and thus have the bonds of love tied in the freedom of the Spirit. As far as I can understand, genuine Free Church work will be almost impossible under our conditions without a great many free meetings and gatherings.”

As may be seen from these statements by Professor Sverdrup, he placed great weight upon the people of the Free Church coming together for free meetings.

And it is a well-known fact among us that quite a number of such larger or smaller meetings are also held, at which it is desired that brothers and sisters from several congregations should gather. This is both necessary and right; for we shall scarcely be supplied in any other way with that power from on high which we must have, if we are to be able to fulfill our task, than by gathering for such meetings.

But I, and perhaps many with me, have observed with concern that the felt need to come together for annual meetings, mission meetings, district meetings, and so forth seems to be declining to an alarming degree. What can be the cause? Could it perhaps be the same cause for which the congregation and the angel of the congregation in Laodicea had to hear these solemn words: “Because you say, ‘I am rich and have abundance and lack nothing,’ and you do not know that you are wretched and pitiable and poor and blind and naked, I therefore counsel you to buy from me—”?

Oh, let us follow his counsel: “buy from him.”
